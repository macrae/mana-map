THE PLAN

NINE MANA, NEVER PAID

What kind of deck builds around a commander it casts in 13.5% of games? One that figured out the commander was never the point: his discount works from the command zone starting turn zero, and the payoff layer does the killing.

Start by unlearning the obvious read: The Ur-Dragon is not the plan. The goldfish simulator (seed 42, 10,000 iterations, conservative model — cost reducers not simulated) puts his mean cast on turn 8.989, with a 0% cast-by-turn-6 rate and a cast at all by turn 10 in just 13.5% of runs. What IS the plan is his Eminence line, which works from the command zone starting on turn zero: every other Dragon spell you cast costs {1} less, all game, without him ever leaving home. Stack that with Dragonlord's Servant, Dragonspeaker Shaman, Herald's Horn, and Urza's Incubator and a nine-drop curve plays like a six-drop curve — which is the entire architectural bet this deck makes.

The engine that actually kills people is the enters-and-attacks payoff layer. Dragon Tempest, Scourge of Valkas, and Terror of the Peaks turn each Dragon entering into targeted damage; Garruk's Uprising and Temur Ascendancy turn big bodies into cards; Miirym, Sentinel Wyrm turns every nontoken Dragon into two. This is a critical-mass deck: below the threshold you have undersized, expensive bodies, and past it every new Dragon draws, burns, and copies itself. The verified stacks show the payoff math — one Ancient Gold Dragon connection under Dragon Tempest produced fifteen tokens and up to 255 points of divisible damage (stack 001), and a single Lathliss cast under Miirym plus Panharmonicon produced two extra Lathlisses and 64 damage across twelve triggers (stack 002).

Your seat for the first five turns is control-slash-value, whether you like it or not: the deck averages 4.95 available mana and 1.54 bodies at turn 6, so it loses every early race. Trade with big blockers, hold the counterspells for spells that actually end the game, and ramp toward the pivot. The pivot window is concrete: a haste enabler on the table (Dragon Tempest or Temur Ascendancy) plus two or three Dragons deployable in one turn — or Tiamat or the commander resolving. That turn you flip to beatdown, and every passive turn afterward is a free draw step for three opponents. Deploy in waves rather than all at once: across a four-player game a wrath is near-certain, and this deck keeps all its card advantage on permanents. The protection package — Teferi's Protection, Heroic Intervention, Flawless Maneuver, Eerie Interlude, plus Patriarch's Bidding as the rebuild — is held by turn 6 in 66% of games; hold one before you commit the wave.

One mana warning the deck-facts brief flags explicitly: six of your sources — Haven of the Spirit Dragon, Maelstrom of the Spirit Dragon, Unclaimed Territory, Rivaz of the Claw, Sarkhan, Fireblood, and Klauth's attack mana — are restricted to casting spells. An activated ability is not a spell, so none of that mana can ever pay for Dragon's Hoard draws, Lathliss pumps, or Haven's own recursion. It accelerates casting Dragons and nothing else; count your unrestricted mana separately when planning an ability-heavy turn.

MULLIGAN FOR FUNCTION

The sim keeps 76% of first sevens, so most hands are fine — the drill is spotting which of these four is the trap. Rule on each before you read the verdict, and remember: the commander is the one card you never mulligan toward.

Keepable first sevens (simulated)76%

KEEP — the textbook

Forest, Mountain, Steam Vents, Urza's Incubator, Farseek, Dragon Tempest, Drakuseth, Maw of Flames

Three lands, ramp, an early payoff — and a cost reducer, the acceleration that matters most. The reducer-plus-ramp hand assembles by turn 6 in only 29.4% of games; when you are dealt one, you do not ship it hunting prettier Dragons.

SHIP — all top, no bridge

Island, Swamp, Mountain, Forest, Command Tower, Tiamat, Dracogenesis

Five lands and two haymakers is the classic trap seven: nothing to do until turn five, no reducer, no payoff, no protection. Lands plus seven-drops with no bridge is the named ship in this deck's mulligan rule.

KEEP — two lands, full engine

Stomping Ground, Mountain, Sol Ring, Dragonspeaker Shaman, Miirym, Sentinel Wyrm, Heroic Intervention, Scourge of Valkas

Two lands rides on Sol Ring, and the rest is a deck in miniature: the deepest discount, the doubler, a payoff, and held protection — which the sim says you want in hand for the commit turn (66.4% of games have it by turn 6).

SHIP — the colours can't cast it

Mountain, Mountain, Clifftop Retreat, Counterspell, Patriarch's Bidding, Miirym, Sentinel Wyrm, Roiling Dragonstorm

Three lands, zero blue or black sources — and this hand is blue-and-black shaped. Five colours is a real tax: black has just 11 land sources against Bidding's {B}{B}, and a hand whose colours cannot cast its spells is a mulligan no matter how good the spells are.

Keep hands with two to four lands and at least one piece of acceleration — and treat cost reducers as the acceleration that matters most. The goldfish run is blunt about this: some ramp or fixing is drawn by turn 6 in 75.6% of games, but a cost reducer in only 42.9%, and the reducers, not the land drops, are what compress a nine-drop curve into a castable one (land drops decay to 50.1% by turn 6). A hand with Dragonspeaker Shaman, Urza's Incubator, or Herald's Horn is a keep almost regardless of what else it holds; the simulator's 'turn-5 Ur-Dragon hand' (reducer plus ramp) assembles by turn 6 in only 29% of games, so when you're dealt one, do not ship it hunting for prettier Dragons. The second thing to want is an early payoff — Dragon Tempest, Garruk's Uprising, Roiling Dragonstorm — because the engine pays per Dragon and wants to be down before the first one lands. What you never mulligan for is the commander: Eminence works from the command zone, so a hand that casts him is worth nothing extra. Ship hands that are all lands and seven-drops with no bridge, and hands whose colours can't cast their spells — five colours is a real tax, black is the thinnest at 11 land sources, and the six spells-only sources (Haven, Maelstrom, Unclaimed Territory, Rivaz, Sarkhan) only count toward casting Dragons, not toward abilities or non-Dragon spells. A protection spell (Teferi's Protection, Heroic Intervention, Flawless Maneuver) is a genuine keep-bonus — held protection is what lets you commit the wave later — and the simulator's 76% keep-7 rate says most sevens are fine; mulligan for function, not for glamour.

What do you do with a 38-card sideboard that is all Dragons? Judge every one. Two promotes, one bracket dial, three spares that were never legal options — and an obsolescence index that mostly concedes the deck already upgraded itself.

The upgrade path lives in the deck's own 38-card sideboard, and the sideboard analysis sorts it cleanly: mostly a bench of interchangeable Dragon bodies plus a spellslinger package that belongs to a different deck, hiding four genuinely differentiated cards. Two are unconditional promotes. Deflecting Swat comes in for An Offer You Can't Refuse: free only while a commander you control is actually on the battlefield — Eminence works from the command zone and does not switch it on, so expect to pay {2}{R} until the turn-nine landing, after which it is free exactly when The Ur-Dragon becomes the table's removal magnet — it retargets the removal aimed at The Ur-Dragon or the counterspell aimed at Tiamat, and it hits abilities, which nothing in the 99 does; the Offer answered the same threats for {U} but handed its controller two Treasures on the way out. Korlessa, Scale Singer comes in for Dragonlord's Servant: with 21 Dragon-typed cards, Path of Ancestry's scry, and Herald's Horn's upkeep peek to rig her top card, a two-mana engine that casts Dragons off the library simply outworks the deck's seventh cost-reduction effect — and she is herself a Dragon for the typal payoffs. Neither swap moves the bracket floor.

The third swap is a dial, not a default: Aggravated Assault in for Crucible of Fire, only at tables that have agreed to bracket 4, because the computed floor moves from 3 to 4 the moment Assault is in the list. The Savage Ventmaw loop it enables is checker-verified (stack 003) — this is a real, unbounded win, not a combo-database rumour — while Crucible's +3/+3 is the right cut because the deck wins on triggers, not on stats. Sneak Attack stays benched by default: against counter-heavy pods it lands Drakuseth without casting, but in normal pods it wastes the Eminence discount the deck is built around. Three sideboard cards — Old Gnawbone, Utvara Hellkite, Encroaching Dragonstorm — are duplicate copies of maindeck cards: spares for wear and foiling, never legal swaps.

One reassurance from the obsolescence index: run against this list, it mostly argues the deck already upgraded itself. Klauth's listed successor is Savage Ventmaw — in the deck. Skyshroud Claim's listed replacements are Farseek and Nature's Lore — in the deck, alongside it, because ramp redundancy is the strategy. Temur Ascendancy's flagged successor is Garruk's Uprising — in the deck, stacked with it, since the draw layer wants both triggers. The index's suggestions for Crux of Fate (Extinction Event and friends) are worth ignoring entirely: none of the listed cards can destroy all non-Dragon creatures while sparing your board, and that one-sided mode is the whole reason Crux holds the slot.
