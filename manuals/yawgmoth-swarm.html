TURNS ONE TO FOUR

DRAW FIRST. DRAIN SECOND.

Why does a deck holding eleven infinite lines want you spending turn four drawing cards? Because the engine turns up on its own in three games out of four, and the kills only turn up when you go and fetch them.

Start with the card everyone misreads. Yawgmoth is a draw engine that happens to own a kill. "Pay 1 life, Sacrifice another creature: Put a -1/-1 counter on up to one target creature and draw a card" costs no mana at all — the price is a life and a body, so it runs at instant speed, on any turn, under any tax anybody at the table can name. He hits the battlefield on turn four in 7,249 of 10,000 goldfish runs, mean 4.283, and he is down by turn six in 91.7% of them.

Here is the part that should change how you sit down. The engine is not tutor-dependent. By turn 10 you have renewable fodder in 73.6% of games unassisted and 90.3% with a tutor allowed to fill the gap, a free sacrifice outlet in 73.8% / 90.0%, an ignition source in 73.0% / 90.2%, and a drain in 66.9% / 87.7%. Read every one of those as a pair — the first number excludes tutors, rituals and cost reducers entirely. Three quarters of your games hand you the engine off the top of your library. Pace for that game, because it is the median one.

What the five tutors buy is the finish, and you own four of them. Blowfly Infestation plus Nest of Scarabs goes 2.6% to 32.1% by turn 10. The Mikaeus package goes 1.7% to 26.2%. Exquisite Blood plus South Wind Avatar goes 2.9% to 32.5%. Warren Soultrader plus Gravecrawler plus any drain goes 1.1% to 20.0%. Those four are independent and nothing stops two of them showing up in the same game, so the honest quantity is their union — and the union is not reported. It is strictly greater than 32.5%. The sheet's composite line spans only two of the four; it is not your kill rate, so do not carry it to a table as one.

Now the number that decides your turns. A fully assembled engine does not have one drain rate — it has two, and they are nearly a factor of two apart. Yawgmoth plus Nest of Scarabs plus a death-metering drain is seven drains, 7 per opponent, 28 across the pod, and seven is a proven ceiling. Put Mirkwood Bats in that same slot on that same board and it is thirteen, 13 per opponent, 52 across the pod. Count the bodies before you start, not during. And remember what Grave Pact does to the same chain: every creature of yours that dies makes each other player sacrifice one, so the drain is the visible clock and the edicts are the half that actually wins fair games.

The dream pair with nothing under it. Stack 001 proved that loop unbounded and then named its price — the drain plus two other creatures on the battlefield when it starts. The rest of this hand costs four and six.

The keep test is not "do I have the kill." It is: can this hand make bodies, and can it kill one of them for free? Every line in the deck routes through those two classes, and they are the two the sim finds earliest — mean turn 2.796 for renewable fodder and 2.803 for a free outlet.

You get to be picky, and you almost never have to be. The goldfish keeps its first seven 82.3% of the time at a mean of 0.209 mulligans, and its kept hands cluster hard: 3,586 with three lands, 3,422 with two, 2,226 with four. Two to five lands is your band. Below two you are hoping; above five you are drawing air, because 48 of the 64 spells cost three or less and mean available mana on turn four is 3.910.

Keep on sight: two to four lands plus something that renews a body. Bitterblossom on turn two is the best keep in the deck — a Faerie every upkeep for 1 life, no card spent, and it rebuilds through a sweeper by itself. Reassembling Skeleton and Gravecrawler are the same promise from the graveyard. Nest of Scarabs, Grave Titan, Sedgemoor Witch and Black Market Connections round out the class, and you have one of them by turn 10 in 73.6% of games unassisted, 90.3% with a tutor.

Also keep any hand with Sol Ring, Ancient Tomb or Dark Ritual and a black source. Fast mana is the thinnest thing on the sheet — 43.5% unassisted, 78.2% assisted — so a hand that already holds it is ahead of a curve most of your games never get. That is the hand that casts Yawgmoth on turn two.

Ship the inverse hand without a second thought. Four lands and three payoffs with no fodder and no outlet is a mulligan, however good the cards read: strategy:mulligans.engine-hands is blunt that payoffs without the enabler are blanks until the engine exists, while enablers at least dig toward the payoff. Ship a hand whose only sacrifice outlet is the commander you cannot cast until turn four. Ship five and six lands. And use the free first mulligan ruthlessly — 100-card singleton means you are mulliganing to enabler classes, not to named cards, and this deck built the redundancy for it.

COMMIT FIRST

THREE PAYOFFS, FIVE OPEN LANDS

Grave Pact is on the battlefield, three payoffs are in your hand, and the Azorius seat has held five untapped lands through two rotations. Pick one before you read a word of the Coach's answer.

You cannot kill anybody this turn. So stop asking which payoff is biggest and start asking which one is still there next turn.

Grave Pact is down, three payoffs are in hand, and Player 3 has five lands open — which one hits the table?

You Turn six, your precombat main, 30 life. Battlefield: Yawgmoth, Thran Physician (2/4, untapped, no summoning sickness — he came down on turn four, which the goldfish does in 7,249 of 10,000 games), Nest of Scarabs, Grave Pact, Bitterblossom, three 1/1 black Insect tokens and one 1/1 black Faerie Rogue token with flying. Five lands, all untapped; the sim's mean available mana on turn six is 5.228, so you are exactly on curve. Two things about that board matter more than anything else on it. First, every body you control is a TOKEN. Second, you own no drain — nothing on your side converts a death into life loss, so the engine you have been running for two turns has removed zero life from anybody. It has drawn you cards and, since Grave Pact landed, it has been quietly eating the table's creatures.
Hand Mirkwood Bats, Bastion of Remembrance, Blowfly Infestation

You cannot kill anyone this turn from this board, and Player 3 untaps into a live sweeper. So which of the three payoffs goes down — the biggest number, the safest number, or the famous one? Or none of them, and you simply take the edicts?

Cast Blowfly Infestation and ignite the chain

The line
Cast Blowfly Infestation for {2}{B}, leaving {B}{B}. Activate Yawgmoth: pay 1 life, sacrifice an Insect, put the counter on another of your own Insects. That Insect dies, Blowfly triggers, the counter moves to the next body, and the chain runs — with Grave Pact out, every one of those deaths makes Player 2 and Player 4 sacrifice a creature.
Signals sent
The loudest card in the 99, deployed on the turn it removes zero life. Blowfly Infestation sitting beside Nest of Scarabs is the exact picture this table was warned about in the pregame, and Player 3 has five lands open to answer the picture.
Coalition risk
The highest available to you, and it buys perception rather than position. You would be showing the combo face without the combo, into the one seat holding interaction, on a turn when nobody's life total moves. That is how a table decides the quiet black deck is the archenemy and then acts on it for three consecutive turns.
Read

The card is a closer. This is not a closing turn.

Start with what the chain actually removes. You control no each-opponent drain, so the loop takes zero life off anybody. Stack 001 already measured the near-miss version: with the drain as the only spare body, the whole unbounded engine drained exactly 3 per opponent. Here there is no drain at all, so the figure is zero.

What you would really be buying is the edict chain, and I want to be straight with you about its status. Grave Pact running under this engine is not a resolved line — the deck's own frame files it as needing a stack scenario, and I will not hand you a table-wide board wipe as a fact when nobody has checked the count.

You also do not need Blowfly to get edicts. Grave Pact already pays you one round of them per activation, for free, with the four tokens you are holding.

And mind the targeting. Blowfly's trigger is mandatory and it demands a target; once your spare bodies run down, the counter lands on whatever is left standing, which is how stack 007's version of this loop ate its own payoff. An ability with no legal choice is simply removed from the stack — that is your only exit, and exiting is not a plan.

My call, and it is judgement: Blowfly goes down when a drain is already on the battlefield and you can count the iterations out loud. Tonight it is a three-mana enchantment that announces you and does nothing.

Cast Mirkwood Bats and take the biggest measured number

The line
Cast Mirkwood Bats for {3}{B}, tapping four of your five lands. Pass with one land up. From next turn, every Yawgmoth activation paid with a token pays Bats twice — once for the sacrifice, once for the Insect Nest of Scarabs creates.
Signals sent
A four-mana flier on an empty-handed turn, into five open lands. It reads as a threat before it reads as a payoff, and it taps you out to do it. Player 3 learns that you were holding something and that you are no longer holding mana.
Coalition risk
High, and mistimed rather than wrong. Bats is the one payoff shape every deck at this table already has an answer for — a creature. Your other two payoffs are enchantments. Presenting the creature first spends your deflection budget on the piece that is cheapest to remove.
Read

The number is real and it is the best one you own. On the like-for-like board, Bats is thirteen triggers where a death-metering drain is seven — stack 008 against stack 003, 13 per opponent against 7, an 86% margin. On a clear turn, Bats is the card.

This is not a clear turn, and the reason is mechanical rather than political.

Bats reads 'Whenever you create or sacrifice a token.' A sweeper destroys; it does not sacrifice. To sacrifice a permanent, its controller moves it from the battlefield to the graveyard on an instruction — nobody instructs anything when a wrath resolves. Stack 007 settled the general form of this on a token that died to a state-based action: not sacrificed, so Bats collects nothing.

So run the bad case. Player 3 wraths, your four tokens and your Bats die, and Bats pays you exactly zero. You spent four mana and your best payoff to lose both.

Then count what you gave up to do it. Four lands tapped means no {B}{B} for the proliferate, which is worth a full extra drain on the verified board.

Bats is next turn's card, or the card after the sweeper is spent. Sequence it late, on purpose. You are not passing on the thirteen — you are collecting it on a turn where nothing takes it away from you.

Cast Bastion of Remembrance and keep {B}{B} up

The line
Cast Bastion of Remembrance for {2}{B}. Its enters trigger makes a 1/1 white Human Soldier token, so you end the turn on five bodies rather than four. Hold {B}{B} for Yawgmoth's proliferate and do nothing else on your own turn. From this point every creature you control that dies drains each opponent for 1 and gains you 1 — whether you sacrificed it, whether a -1/-1 counter killed it, or whether Player 3 wraths it away.
Signals sent
Modest, and modest is a lie you are allowed to tell. An enchantment and a 1/1 token, cast with two mana still up, beside a board that has been making people sacrifice creatures all game. Player 3 has to decide whether the two open lands are a trick, and you have not shown them a creature to point removal at.
Coalition risk
Moderate and falling. This is the payoff that does not present a target: creature removal cannot touch an enchantment, and the drain arrives without giving the table a new thing to kill. The edicts stay the visible half of your board, and edicts read as fair.
Read

This is the branch, and the reason is not the size of the number. It is which number survives contact with the seat holding five lands.

Bastion meters DEATHS. That is precisely why it is right into open sweeper mana and Mirkwood Bats is not. Dying is being put into a graveyard from the battlefield, and a wrath does that to everything you own at once.

So run the bad case again. Player 3 wraths. Your four tokens and the Human Soldier die — five deaths, five drains, five life from every seat and five back to you, paid for by their card. Bastion is an enchantment and is still there afterwards. Yawgmoth is a sixth drain only if you let him hit the graveyard; sending him to the command zone means he never died, and the cheaper recast is usually worth more than the point.

Better still, do not wait. Yawgmoth's ability costs no mana and you may activate it any time you have priority. Hold priority when the sweeper goes on the stack and convert every token into a card and a Bastion drain before it resolves.

Now the trap this board is really teaching, because Grave Pact changes what an activation costs. Yawgmoth's ability becomes the topmost object on the stack when you announce it, and the cost — the life and the body — is paid after that. So your token's death triggers Grave Pact while your ability is still sitting there, and nothing happens at the moment an ability triggers: it goes on the stack the next time a player would get priority, which puts it ABOVE your ability. The edicts resolve first.

Read what that means. If you targeted Player 4's 2/1 and Player 4 chooses that same 2/1 for the edict, your ability has no legal target when it tries to resolve, so it does not resolve at all. No counter, no Insect, no card — and you already paid the life and the body. They get to choose. Assume they choose well.

The fix is written into your commander. 'Up to one target creature' lets you choose ZERO targets, and an ability with no target cannot be fizzled by their choice. You give up the Nest token; take that trade every time the edict can reach what you were aiming at.

The better technique when you want the body too: target one of your own tokens. They cannot sacrifice it, so the card is guaranteed, Nest still pays you an Insect, and the counter-killed token is a second Grave Pact round. It costs you a net body and buys two rounds of edicts.

One political caution, judgement rather than measurement. Player 3 controls no creatures, so every edict you fire hits the two decks that are pressuring Player 3 for you. Spend edicts on the seat that is coming for you, and let the aggro deck keep swinging at somebody else.

Cast nothing, hold all three payoffs, and take the edicts at instant speed

The line
Pass with five lands up and three payoffs in hand. At each opponent's end step, activate Yawgmoth once: pay 1 life, sacrifice an Insect, choose zero targets, draw. Grave Pact makes Player 2 and Player 4 each sacrifice a creature every time. Three activations across the rotation empties both boards and puts three cards in your hand for three life.
Signals sent
Nothing about your hand, and a great deal about your board. Five open lands say interaction; three edicts say the sacrifice deck is running. Player 3 reads the open mana correctly — as a player who did not want to commit — and takes the free turn.
Coalition risk
Low on you and badly aimed. The edicts land on the two seats putting pressure on Player 3, and Player 3 has no creatures to lose. You would spend a full rotation clearing the board for the deck that beats you.
Read

The instinct is right and the execution hands Player 3 the game.

Holding is what a control deck sells you. Buying time is their plan, so a turn where you spend nothing and they spend nothing is a turn they won. Playing safe from ahead is correct; this is playing scared, and it is paying real equity to dodge a card they may not have.

Your five open mana is a bluff, and you should know that when you make it. There is not an instant in your hand. A bluff is a fine thing to run — paying an entire turn for one is not.

Then look at where the edicts go. Player 2 and Player 4 lose creatures; Player 3 loses nothing and keeps drawing. You are doing the control deck's board management for it, with your own life total.

The technique inside this branch is correct and belongs in your hands: zero targets under Grave Pact is exactly right, because it makes the card draw unfizzleable. Right technique, wrong turn.

And here is the part that makes this branch unnecessary. Bastion of Remembrance costs three of your five mana. It does not stop you taking a single one of these end-step activations — it just makes each of them remove life as well as a creature.

Our call: Cast Bastion of Remembrance and keep {B}{B} up

Refuse the framing first. None of these three cards kills anybody tonight, so stop asking which one is biggest and start asking which one is still there on your next turn.

The biggest is Mirkwood Bats and I am not talking you out of it forever. Thirteen triggers against seven on the like-for-like board, stack 008 against stack 003 — 13 from each opponent against 7, an 86% margin, the widest measured gap the deck owns. That card is coming down. It comes down after Player 3's five lands have been spent.

Because the sweeper is the whole question, and the two payoffs answer it in opposite directions. Bats is paid when you CREATE or SACRIFICE a token; a wrath does neither, so it pays Bats nothing. Bastion is paid when a creature you control DIES, and a wrath is nothing but deaths. Cast Bastion and their sweeper becomes five drains — five life from every seat, fifteen across this three-opponent table — on their card, not yours.

Keep the numbers straight while you say it out loud. The verified engine is seven drains, seven from EACH opponent; 28 is the pod total across the four opponents stacks 002 and 003 measured. Three opponents here means 21 across the table, and Player 4 goes from 27 to 20. That is a clock, not a kill, and a clock is what you want tonight.

Hold the {B}{B}. It is Yawgmoth's proliferate, worth a full extra drain on the verified board, and it is a second turn of open mana that Player 3 has to respect.

Then play the activations properly, because Grave Pact rewrote them. The edicts resolve before your counter does, so anything you target can be sacrificed out from under you — and the ability that loses its only target does not resolve, which means no counter, no Insect, and no card after you already paid. Choose zero targets when the edict can reach your target. Target your own token when you want the Insect and a second round of edicts. Both are free, and only one of them is what most pilots do by reflex.

One last read to keep you out of trouble: fire those edicts at the seat that is coming for you. Player 3 owns no creatures, so every edict you spend on Player 2 and Player 4 clears the board for the deck you least want untapping.

You are three quarters of the way to a fully assembled engine with three payoffs in hand and nobody has spent a removal spell on you yet. Put down the one they cannot kill, keep two mana up, and let their sweeper pay you.

THREE OPPONENTS

THE QUIET SEAT NOBODY BELIEVES

You arrive on the table's radar at turn four, because that is when your best card lands. So what do three opponents actually read off your board — and how do you spend that reading instead of resenting it?

You are on the table's radar at turn four, and you should be glad about it — that is how early this engine comes online. The goldfish puts Yawgmoth on the battlefield on turn four in 7,249 of 10,000 games, mean cast turn 4.283, down by turn six in 91.7% of them. Nothing else you do is that reliable. Your best card and your first tell arrive together, so plan the whole game around that one fact.

Know exactly what three opponents read off you. The bracket report puts you at floor 4 on four Game Changers — Ancient Tomb, Demonic Tutor, Necropotence, Vampiric Tutor — eleven two-card infinites, and 42 combo lines fully contained in the 99. Add five tutors and, from across the table, you look like a combo deck wearing a sacrifice costume.

That perception is a resource, not a problem. strategy:multiplayer.threat-deflection tells you the shape of it: "combo decks look non-threatening while advancing in secret, which is exactly why experienced tables hit the quiet ramp player." Read in reverse, that sentence is a description of your seat. Your quiet turns will be discounted by anyone who tracks draw engines, so spend the discount deliberately rather than hoping for it.

Now hold the number that keeps everything manageable. A fully assembled drain engine — Yawgmoth, Nest of Scarabs, a death-metering drain — produces seven drains. Stack 002 proves it with Bastion of Remembrance, stack 003 proves it with Zulaport Cutthroat, both checker-pass, both landing on seven per opponent and 28 across a four-opponent pod. The table goes from 40 to 33.

So a scary board is a clock, and the table will price it as a kill. That is my read of this deck's central political problem, and it is a sequencing problem — which is the good kind, because you solve those for free.

Two board states genuinely make you the archenemy, and both reads are correct. Mirkwood Bats on the Yawgmoth engine is thirteen triggers, 13 per opponent, 52 across the pod, 160 opposing life down to 108 (stack 008, verified five ways). Blowfly Infestation beside Nest of Scarabs with a death-metering drain and two spare bodies is the real thing — stack 001 puts four opponents from 40 to 0 in 38 completed iterations. Present either only on a turn you can act.

Grave Pact changes the room faster than any drain, and this is the political read I most want you to carry. "Whenever a creature you control dies, each other player sacrifices a creature of their choice." Stack 002's verified board kills seven of your creatures in one engine turn — seven edicts pointed at every other seat.

That extrapolation is mine, not a checked line: the seven deaths are verified, Grave Pact reads off exactly that event, and no stack has run the pair. Price it anyway. A drain takes life quietly and privately; an edict takes a creature from three players in front of everyone, every turn, forever.

So build your read hierarchy before you sit down. Bitterblossom, Reassembling Skeleton, Gravecrawler, Stitcher's Supplier and Sedgemoor Witch are furniture — they read as a fair sacrifice deck and eat almost no removal. Nest of Scarabs, Grave Pact, Mirkwood Bats, Mikaeus and Exquisite Blood are the cards that change the room.

Mikaeus deserves his own paragraph, because he is your biggest tell. He is the shared half of seven of the eleven two-card infinites on the bracket report — Ashnod's Altar, Phyrexian Altar, Carrion Feeder, Viscera Seer, Woe Strider, Yahenni, Blasting Station. The table's correct answer to this deck is to exile him on sight. Do not teach them that.

Do not lean on him harder than the evidence does, either. Undying returns the body with a +1/+1 counter on it, which almost certainly means the loop has to route through a Yawgmoth -1/-1 to clear that counter — a three-card line, not a two-card one. None of the seven is verified; each needs a stack scenario. Treat Mikaeus as a superb value engine you would like to untap with, and let the table's fear of him do the rest.

Exquisite Blood is the opposite kind of card: it does nothing visible and it is the one nobody sees coming. With South Wind Avatar it is the highest-bracket line the report names — a candidate, not a fact, and it needs a stack scenario before you call it a kill. Deploy the Avatar first, because a 3/4 deathtouch body reads as fair. The Blood goes down on the turn it converts.

That is the general rule: lead fodder, hold the hub. Begin with the clause that makes this format unlike every other one. CR 903.8: "A player may cast a commander they own from the command zone." Yawgmoth is therefore not a card you draw. He is a card you have. The simulation puts him on the battlefield in 9,847 of 10,000 games, mean turn 4.283, and 7,249 of those cast him on turn four exactly.

The exhibit beside it is the price of the guarantee, and it compounds. The same rule adds "an additional {2} for each previous time the player casting it has cast it from the command zone that game." Four mana, then six, then eight, then ten. Set that schedule against mean available mana of 3.910 on turn four and 7.103 on turn ten. The plain-English holding is short. The first recast costs you a turn. The second costs you the whole turn. The third is a turn nobody is going to give you.

Colour identity is CR 903.4, and it is the constraint that built the other ninety-eight cards. One black symbol in the cost, one colour in the deck — and the audit comes back clean: black is 100% of the pip load and 100% of the sources, on curve at 96.0% once ramp is counted. What it costs is breadth. This library holds no counterspell, no artifact removal and no enchantment removal, and none of the five tutors can find one, because there is none to find. That is the format's bargain stated as a rule rather than as a complaint.

Commander damage next, because the record disposes of it quickly. CR 903.10a removes a player who has been dealt 21 or more combat damage by the same commander. Yawgmoth is a 2/4 who would very much rather be sacrificing than attacking, and twenty-one damage is eleven connections. Commander damage is a coincidence in this deck, not a clock.

Now the clause to memorise, because it cuts in both directions. CR 702.16b: "A permanent or player with protection can't be targeted by spells with the stated quality and can't be targeted by abilities from a source with the stated quality." Yawgmoth has protection from Humans and is himself a Human Cleric, so his own ability can never target him. Read that twice — it is the exact clause that bounds every engine in The Kill, because the 2/4 who could comfortably absorb four counters is not allowed to take one. In the other direction it is free armour in a format thick with Human commanders, and CR 702.16f keeps Human creatures from blocking him besides.

One last fact about the zone before the corner office speaks. Three opponents can see it. They cannot see your hand. What they price when this commander resolves is a reputation rather than a board — and the bracket sheet agrees with them, landing at floor 4 on four Game Changers and eleven two-card infinites.

COACH'S READ. One decision, and you make it early: buy the insurance before the removal, never after. Lightning Greaves equips at sorcery speed only, so the turn to arm him is the turn before you need him. Malakir Rebirth and Supernatural Stamina both return him to the battlefield rather than to the zone, which means the tax never advances at all — they are the cheapest recasts in the deck precisely because they are not recasts. And price the second trip before you make the first: six mana spent getting him back is six mana not spent on the hub. Then remember what he is for. Use him as a draw engine for three or four turns before you ever use him as a kill.

THE ONLY TEN

BUY THE MANA, THEN BUY THE BRANCH

Ten cards, ranked, and not one of them names Mikaeus. Two are free stock sitting in another box; the rest are the cheapest way to stop seven of your eleven infinites resting on a single body.

Rank a slot by what it moves, and on this deck three numbers do the moving. Start with the widest one: a creation-side payoff is worth 13 drains and 52 pod life where a death-metering payoff is worth 7 and 28 on the identical board. That is verified, it is an 86% gap, and only two cards in the 99 collect on the creation side. Anything that pays when a token arrives — rather than when one dies — is buying the biggest measured margin the deck has.

The second number is fast mana, which sits at 43.5% unassisted and 78.2% assisted by turn 10 and is the thinnest class on the sheet by a distance. Three cards carry it. The commander costs {2}{B}{B} and lands on turn two in 707 of 10,000 games; every additional turn-one accelerant moves that histogram directly. Engine mana is the same argument one notch up: 60.6% and 85.0%, five cards, and it is what converts a wide board into a castable turn rather than a parked one.

The third is not a percentage, it is a shape. Nest of Scarabs is a singleton hub, it is an enchantment, and nothing in the 99 rebuys an enchantment — Phyrexian Reclamation, Persist, Victimize, Animate Dead and Agadeem's Awakening all read creature card. So a slot that closes on an axis independent of the Nest is worth more than its raw power suggests, and that is why the deck runs four named kills rather than one.

Watch what will not move, because it sets the ceiling on the conversation. The bracket floor is 4, driven by four Game Changers and eleven two-card infinites, and seven of those eleven name one card. A slot that extends an engine does not escalate the deck; a slot that adds a twelfth two-card infinite does not either, because the floor is already there. What actually raises the deck's power is verification, not addition — the same checker that measured the 13 has also refuted three lines the combo database called kills.

The engine is fed; the mana and the payoff surface are not. SEVENTY-THREE HANDS, NO HEADLINER

There is no standout in this deck and we are not going to invent one. The deepest credit here is two cards and twelve artists are tied at exactly that — so the feature goes to the hand that painted the face of the issue.

Start with the number that decides this section. Eighty-five cards counted, seventy-three different signatures, and the deepest single credit is two cards — twelve artists are tied there. Nobody dominates this deck, so nobody is going to be told they did.

Mark Winters' Yawgmoth is the Dominaria Remastered printing: the physician mid-experiment, lit from below, more surgeon than devil. It is the right read of a card whose rules text is a laboratory. Pay a life, spend a body, learn something.

One figure needs saying out loud because it is the one that lies. Jorge Jacinto's Swamp appears twenty-two times in this deck and counts once. Authorship is per card, never per copy — count it the other way and a basic land wins this section every single issue.

The printings cluster where recent Commander product does: fifteen cards from Lorwyn Eclipsed Commander, eight from Secrets of Strixhaven Commander, seven from Tarkir: Dragonstorm Commander, six from Commander Masters. Nobody curated that, and the analysis is specific about it — there is no contiguous collector-number run anywhere in the deck. This is not a drop bought whole. It is simply where the cardboard comes from, and it is most of why no single hand gets ahead.

And nothing here is foil and nothing is borderless. Eighty-two black borders and three white ones left over from Fifth and Sixth Edition. This is a working deck, sleeved at working prices, and it looks like one on purpose.
