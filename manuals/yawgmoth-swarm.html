THE GAME PLAN

PAID IN BODIES, NOT MANA

Your commander draws a card for no mana at all, at instant speed, on any turn you like. So why do two games in three end with no kill in sight — and why is that exactly fine?

Here is the first thing to get right: Yawgmoth is a draw engine wearing a kill's reputation. "Pay 1 life, Sacrifice another creature: Put a -1/-1 counter on up to one target creature and draw a card" costs no mana at all. The price is life and bodies, and you pay it at instant speed, on any turn, under any tax. He is on the battlefield by turn six in 91.3% of goldfish games, mean turn 4.287, and 7,208 of 10,000 runs cast him exactly on four.

Now the number that organises your whole game. By turn 10 this deck assembles one of its two kills in 4.6% of games on its own draws, and in 31.4% once the five tutors are allowed to fetch the missing half. Read those together and never apart: 4.6% is a floor that excludes tutors, rituals and cost reducers, and 31.4% is the estimate. That 27-point gap is what Demonic Tutor, Vampiric Tutor, Beseech the Mirror, Diabolic Intent and Insatiable Avarice actually buy you, and it is the largest single contribution any five slots make.

So two games in three have no kill, and every game has an engine. Pace for the engine. By turn 10 renewable fodder is in hand 78.6% of the time unassisted and 92.3% tutor-assisted, a free sacrifice outlet 78.0% / 92.1%, an ignition source 73.5% / 90.6%, and a drain 60.8% / 85.4%. Those are a control deck's opening figures in every column, and Yawgmoth converts every one of them into cards nobody can Counterspell.

Then the honest part, because it changes how you aim. Three separate verified stacks measured a fully assembled engine at seven drains — 28 life across a four-player pod, taking the table from 40 to 33. That is a clock, not a kill. Stacks 002, 003 and 005 all land on the same figure by different routes and all end the same way: body exhaustion, not life, not library.

Which means you do not go for the pod. You go for a seat. Count spare bodies before the first activation, add the toughness your board can absorb above 1, and check that number against the lowest life total at the table. An opponent's untapped board is fuel — a 2/2 they control extends the engine by two activations (stack 002) — so the worst board this deck can face is an empty one. Take one player out, keep the cards the attempt drew, and do it again next turn.

TABLE MANNERS

YOUR BEST CARD IS YOUR FIRST TELL

Yawgmoth lands on turn four in 7,208 games out of 10,000, and the table starts its clock the moment he resolves. Here is how to spend the next three turns being underestimated on purpose.

The table starts its clock on you the moment Yawgmoth resolves, and the goldfish says that moment is turn four. Mean cast turn 4.287, exactly turn four in 7,208 of 10,000 games, on the battlefield in 98.6% of them and down by turn six in 91.3%. Nothing else this deck does is that reliable. Plan the whole game around the fact that your best card is also your first tell.

Know what three opponents read off it. The bracket report puts you at floor 4, driven by four Game Changers — Ancient Tomb, Demonic Tutor, Necropotence, Vampiric Tutor — and eight two-card infinites. Add five tutors and, from across the table, you look like a combo deck. strategy:multiplayer.threat-deflection warns that "combo decks look non-threatening while advancing in secret, which is exactly why experienced tables hit the quiet ramp player" — read that in reverse and it is a description of you.

Now hold the number that makes all of this manageable. A fully assembled engine — Yawgmoth, Nest of Scarabs, an each-opponent drain — is seven drains. Stack 002 proves it with Bastion of Remembrance, stack 003 proves it with Zulaport Cutthroat, both checker-pass, both landing on the same figure: seven life from each opponent, 28 across a four-opponent table. The pod goes from 40 to 33.

So you telegraph lethality you do not yet have. That is my read, and it is this deck's central political problem: your scary board is a clock, and the table prices it as a kill. The good news is that it is a sequencing problem, and sequencing problems are the ones you get to solve for free.

The read hierarchy is specific. Bitterblossom, Ophiomancer, Carrier Thrall, Bloodghast and Sedgemoor Witch are furniture — they read as a fair sacrifice deck and they eat almost no removal. Yawgmoth himself reads as a draw engine and a lightning rod; expect to pay for him and price the recast in before you cast him. Nest of Scarabs is the card that changes the room, because it is the hub every verified line routes through and nothing in the 99 rebuys an enchantment.

Two board states genuinely flip you to archenemy, and both reads are correct. Blowfly Infestation beside Nest of Scarabs with a drain and two spare bodies is the real thing — stack 001 proves that loop unbounded and puts four opponents from 40 to 0 in 38 completed iterations. Mikaeus, the Unhallowed is the shared half of seven of the eight two-card infinites on the bracket report. Present either one next to a drain only on a turn you can act.

The rule that follows: lead fodder, hold the hub. Cast Nest of Scarabs on the turn it converts, not the turn you can first afford it. strategy:multiplayer.pivot-window tells you to "sequence the scariest piece last so the deflection budget lasts until the turn you take it" — with a three-card assembly, the last piece is the one everybody would have answered.

Tutors are information, so spend them at the right speed. Vampiric Tutor is an instant: fire it on the end step before your untap and the table sees a card leave your hand, not a plan (strategy:tempo.sequencing prefers "committing at the end step before your own turn"). Demonic Tutor, Diabolic Intent and Beseech the Mirror are sorceries and announce the go turn simply by being cast. The payoff is enormous — either kill assembles by turn ten in 4.6% of games on natural draws and 31.4% once a tutor fetches the missing half — so protect the tempo of that spend as carefully as you choose the target.

The go decision is body arithmetic, done before the first activation rather than during it. Every engine stack that ran to exhaustion ended the same way; stack 005 names it "by body exhaustion." Count spare bodies, then count survivable toughness on top: South Wind Avatar's four toughness is worth exactly +4 activations (stack 005), and Yawgmoth's "{B}{B}, Discard a card: Proliferate" is worth exactly one more drain — 8 and 32 instead of 7 and 28 (stack 002).

Subtract one thing from that count. Ophiomancer makes its Snake only "if you control no Snakes," and South Wind Avatar is a Snake Spirit Avatar. While the Avatar is on the battlefield, your per-upkeep deathtouch body stops arriving. The Avatar is still worth every mana — just do the arithmetic with it, not around it.

Life is your other currency and you spend it constantly: a point per Yawgmoth activation, plus Necropotence, Bitterblossom, Black Market Connections, City of Brass, Ancient Tomb, Cabal Pit and Ifnir Deadlands. strategy:life-as-resource is on your side early — "early points are cheap and late points are priceless" — and the engine pays you back. Stacks 002 and 003 both end with you at 42, above where the turn started.

The payment makes you archenemy in exactly one shape: a visibly low total, no drain on the battlefield, and an untapped combat board across from you. Do not Necropotence into that picture. Track each seat's clock on you separately and stop spending one turn early rather than one turn late.

Then embrace being the threat, because the pivot is smaller than it feels. It is not "can I kill the pod," it is "can I kill one seat" — go when the lowest life total at the table is at or under your spare bodies plus the counters your surviving toughness can absorb. And this engine leaves you rebuilt when it stalls: stack 002 ends with five fresh cards in hand, stack 005 with eight. strategy:multiplayer.pivot-window asks you to "prefer win attempts that leave a rebuilt position if they fail." Yours always does. Take the window.

One last piece of free armour the table forgets: protection from Humans. A Human source cannot target Yawgmoth (CR 702.16b), Human damage to him is prevented (CR 702.16e), and Human creatures cannot block him when he attacks (CR 702.16f) — real text in a format thick with Human commanders. Lightning Greaves is the other insurance, and equip activates only as a sorcery (CR 702.6a), so you buy it the turn before the removal, never in response to it (strategy:multiplayer.commander-insurance).

FIVE WISHES

WISH FOR THE RIGHT DRAIN

Five tutors are worth 27 percentage points on the finish, which is the largest thing any five slots in this 99 do. Spend one on the wrong payoff and you have fetched eight life instead of twenty-eight.

Five wishes, and the sim priced them for you: by turn 10 you assemble one of your two kills in 4.6% of games on your own draws, and in 31.4% once a tutor is allowed to fetch the missing half. That 27-point gap IS these five slots. Nothing else in the 99 buys a number like it, and it is why this section is the deck's instruction manual and not a footnote. Now spend them correctly. The deck finds its own staples without help: renewable fodder is in hand by turn 10 in 78.6% of games unassisted, a free sacrifice outlet in 78.0%, an ignition source in 73.5%, and Yawgmoth himself casts by turn 6 in 91.3% at a mean of turn 4.29. Those classes do not need a wish. Two things do: Nest of Scarabs, the singleton hub every loop routes through, and a drain — the drain class is 60.8% unassisted, the lowest number on the sheet that the engine actually needs. Fetch the RIGHT drain. Carry this one to every table. On the same verified board, Bastion of Remembrance removes 7 life from each opponent, 28 across the pod (stack 002); Zulaport Cutthroat does the same 7 / 28 (stack 003); Blood Artist removes EIGHT LIFE TOTAL (stack 004, checker verdict: not a kill, and not even close). That is a 3.5x penalty for 'target player' over 'each opponent'. Fetching Blood Artist as your payoff is the single most expensive mistake this section can save you from. Begin with the clause that makes this format unlike every other one. CR 903.8: "A player may cast a commander they own from the command zone." Yawgmoth is therefore not a card you draw. He is a card you have. Across ten thousand simulated games he reached the battlefield in 98.6% of them, at a mean of turn 4.287, and 7,208 of those games cast him on turn four exactly.

The exhibit beside this is the price of that guarantee, and it compounds. The same rule adds {2} for each previous cast from the zone: four mana, then six, then eight, then ten. Set that schedule against a deck whose mean available mana is 3.927 on turn four and 7.109 on turn ten. The plain-English holding is short. The first recast costs you a turn. The second costs you the whole turn. The third is a turn nobody is going to give you.

Colour identity is CR 903.4, and it is the constraint that built the other ninety-eight cards. One black symbol in the cost, one colour in the deck — and the audit comes back clean: 100% of the pip load and 100% of the sources are black, on curve at 97.0% once ramp is counted. What it costs is breadth. This library holds no counterspell, no artifact removal and no enchantment removal, and none of the five tutors can find one, because there is none to find. That is the format's bargain, stated as a rule rather than as a complaint.

Commander damage next, because the record disposes of it quickly. CR 903.10a removes a player who has taken 21 combat damage from the same commander. Yawgmoth is a 2/4 who would very much rather be sacrificing than attacking; twenty-one damage is eleven connections. Commander damage is a coincidence in this deck, not a clock.

Now the clause to memorise, and it cuts both ways. CR 702.16b: "A permanent or player with protection can't be targeted by spells with the stated quality and can't be targeted by abilities from a source with the stated quality." Yawgmoth has protection from Humans and is himself a Human Cleric, so his own ability can never target him. Read that twice — it is the exact clause that bounds every engine in The Kill, because the 2/4 who could comfortably absorb four counters is not allowed to take one. In the other direction it is free armour in a format thick with Human commanders, and CR 702.16f keeps Human creatures from blocking him besides.

One last fact about the zone before the corner office speaks. Three opponents can see it. They cannot see your hand. What they price when Yawgmoth resolves is a reputation, not a board — and the bracket report agrees with them, landing at floor 4 on four Game Changers and eight two-card infinites.

COACH'S READ. One decision, and you make it early: buy the insurance before the removal, never after. Lightning Greaves equips at sorcery speed only, so the turn to arm him is the turn before you need it. Malakir Rebirth and Supernatural Stamina both return him to the battlefield rather than to the zone, which means the tax never advances at all — they are the cheapest recasts in the deck because they are not recasts. And price the second trip before you make the first: six mana spent getting him back is six mana not spent on the hub.

THE SHORT LIST

EIGHT OF THESE ARE PURCHASES

Ten cards, ranked, and the honest part goes first: only Black Sun's Zenith and Plumb the Forbidden are already in the box. The other eight are a wish list, and the list is more useful for saying so out loud.

Ten names render below, and the arithmetic that ranks them is the same arithmetic that runs the deck. Every verified engine here ends by body exhaustion — stack 005's final state says it outright, "no creature other than Yawgmoth left to sacrifice - not life, not library" — so a card that renews a body is worth more than a card that adds a payoff. That is the first of the two holes the list answers.

The second is a kill that does not route through Nest of Scarabs. The hub is a singleton with no redundancy in the 99, it is an enchantment, and this deck holds no enchantment removal to trade back with. A finish that closes on a different axis is worth real slots: Exquisite Blood pairs with South Wind Avatar, which is already sleeved, and Reassembling Skeleton turns "{1}{B}: Return this card from your graveyard to the battlefield tapped" into a Yawgmoth activation every single turn.

Watch what the numbers refuse to move. Bracket 4 before, bracket 4 after, on all ten entries — these extend engines, they do not escalate the power level. And every combo line any entry opens carries the same stamp, needs a stack scenario, because the checker has already overturned three Commander Spellbook claims about this exact list: stacks 001, 004 and 005.

Two entries are pure mana rather than pure engine, and they are underrated here. Cabal Coffers reads {2} and a tap for black equal to your Swamps, and 22 of this deck's lands are Swamps — the largest single mana gain available, on a land, costing no spell slot. Grave Pact and Dictate of Erebos fill an effect class the 99 holds none of: a deck that sacrifices a creature nearly every turn converts each activation into an edict aimed at all three opponents.

One caution on the machinery underneath. The obsolescence index flags 13 cards in this 99 as having newer, textually similar analogues, and it scores text similarity plus generic advantages — lower mana value, better toughness, an extra keyword. It does not price the thing this deck actually buys, which is a sacrifice outlet that costs no mana. Read those flags as leads, not as verdicts.

Four bench cards make the ten because each completes a line whose other pieces are already sleeved: Gravecrawler, Reassembling Skeleton, Exquisite Blood, Grave Pact. THE MANA AUDIT

ONE COLOUR, ONE QUESTION

In a mono-black deck the audit cannot argue about which colour, only about how many. Thirty-five of your thirty-six land copies make black, and the 90% yardstick asks for thirty-six.

Start where mono-colour makes the audit easy, then look at the one place it does not. Black carries 100% of the pip load and 100% of the sources — 70.0 pips across 53 cards, up to triple-black in a single cost, first demanded on turn one. Pip share and source share cannot disagree in a deck with one colour, so the interesting question is never which colour, it is how many.

Thirty-six land copies across 15 entries, 22 of them Swamp. Thirty-five of those 36 make black. The yardstick asks for 36 sources and the audit's note is blunt about what that means: in a 36-land deck the target is a yardstick, not a possibility. Lands alone put black on curve at 89.5% — half a point under 90%, and the half point is arithmetic, not a build error.

Ten nonland producers close it. Four rocks, six rituals and two dorks lift the count to 45 sources and on-curve black to 97.0%. Mind which ones score zero: Sol Ring, Ancient Tomb and Ashnod's Altar produce nothing coloured under the audit's unconditional-mana rule, so they buy speed rather than fixing. Restricted mana: none anywhere in the 99.

Four lands enter tapped — Archway Commons, Bojuka Bog, Castle Locthwain and Path of Ancestry — which is the whole tempo cost of the base. The rest is paid in life. City of Brass, Ancient Tomb, Cabal Pit and Ifnir Deadlands all charge you, and they queue behind Necropotence, Black Market Connections, Bitterblossom, Vampiric Tutor and 1 life per Yawgmoth activation.

The development curve is what a 36-land deck looks like when it stops needing lands. That 8 is unreachable, and the proof is by SUPPLY OF BODIES - not by any claim that an 8-activation line sacrifices everything it has, which is FALSE: Insect G is consumed by a counter-kill and never sacrificed at all, which is the whole content of step 7. Suppose instead, for contradiction, that all 8 sacrifices drained. Each would then have to be the sacrifice of a counterless INSECT made while the Avatar was still on the battlefield - the Avatar itself can never be one of them, since its own death does not trigger its own 'another creature you control dies' ability (step 8). Now count how many Insect bodies can exist under that supposition. You start with 2 (A and B). Every further Insect is a Nest token, one per -1/-1 counter placed, and a counter placed on one of your own Insects is exactly body-neutral - it kills one and creates one (step 7) - so only counters placed on Grizzly Bears or on the Avatar can grow the supply. Bears absorbs at most 2. The Avatar can have absorbed at most 3 while still being on the battlefield, because a fourth counter kills it (step 8) and the supposition requires it alive at every one of the 8 sacrifices. That is at most 2 + 3 = 5 supply-growing counters, hence at most 5 tokens, hence at most 2 + 5 = 7 Insect bodies exist in time to be sacrificed - one short of the 8 the supposition demands. Contradiction, so at most 7 drains. The count is unchanged if proliferate places some of those counters (step 10), since they come out of the same fixed 2 + 4 absorption pool. The case split says the same thing concretely: in case [B] the Avatar leaves by being sacrificed and that sacrifice is its own death, which does not trigger it; in case [A] it is counter-killed, and the Nest token made by the killing counter is created after the Avatar is already in the graveyard (step 8) - a body that, being a token from a counter-kill rather than the last body standing, still has to be disposed of with no Avatar on the battlefield. Either way at least one of the 8 sacrifices is drainless, which is what the supply count proves in general - and the line in steps 1-9 attains 7, simultaneously with 8 activations.
    (b) YES, the 4-toughness body extends the bound, by exactly +4 activations. South Wind Avatar contributes 4 of the 6 non-Insect counter slots (+4), adds itself as a sacrificeable body (+1), and loses itself as a body to the very counter that kills it (-1): net +4. Without it, the ledger reads 2 starting Insects + 2 Grizzly Bears counters = 4 activations, and ZERO drains, since it is also the only source of lifegain on the board - so it supplies 100% of the damage. The library is not the constraint either: 8 draws against a stated 60-card library, where the rule that punishes an empty library would need roughly 60 activations against a hard ceiling of 8.
    CR 601.2h — “Partial payments are not allowed. Unpayable costs can’t be paid.”
    CR 104.3c — “If a player is required to draw more cards than are left in their library, they draw the remaining cards and then lose the game the next time a player would receive priority.”
  12. Tally. South Wind Avatar's first ability triggered 8 times over the run: seven sacrifices of counterless 1/1 Insects (last known toughness 1, so 1 life each) plus one counter-killed Insect at toughness 0 (0 life). Its second ability triggered 7 times, once per nonzero lifegain event, draining 1 from each of four opponents each time.
    (a) 8 completed activations, ended by having no creature other than Yawgmoth left to sacrifice. The Avatar's drain DOES fire - 7 times, not 8 and not 10 - for 7 x 4 = 28 total opposing life. Each opponent goes 40 -> 33; 160 opposing life becomes 132. You end at 39 (40 - 8 paid + 7 gained), having drawn 8 cards (library 52). This is decisively NOT infinite and not near-infinite: the deaths that would have to be free are the counter-killed Insects, and those gain 0 life and drain nothing. Commander Spellbook's 'Near-infinite' tag is wrong for this board.
    CR 119.3 — “If an effect causes a player to gain life or lose life, that player’s life total is adjusted accordingly.”

Case A-006 Persist + Nest of Scarabs: does a creature that ENTERS with a -1/-1 counter trigger the Nest?
Rules version June 19, 2026 · status: cleared in 2 review cycle(s) · tap to open Verified
  1. Persist resolves. Its one target is rechecked for legality first: Fume Spitter is still a nonlegendary creature card in your graveyard, so the target is legal and the spell resolves. You follow its instruction in the order written: return Fume Spitter from your graveyard to the battlefield with a -1/-1 counter on it. Persist does not name a controller, so Fume Spitter enters under YOUR control (you are the player the effect instructed to put it onto the battlefield). Persist itself is then put into your graveyard as the final part of its resolution.
    Fume Spitter is on the battlefield under your control with one -1/-1 counter on it. Persist is in your graveyard. Nothing has been targeted or damaged; life totals are unchanged at 40 apiece.
    CR 608.2b — “If the spell or ability specifies targets, it checks whether the targets are still legal.”
    CR 608.2c — “The controller of the spell or ability follows its instructions in the order written.”
    CR 110.2a — “If an effect instructs a player to put an object onto the battlefield, that object enters the battlefield under that player’s control unless the effect states otherwise.”
    CR 608.2n — “As the final part of an instant or sorcery spell’s resolution, the spell is put into its owner’s graveyard.”
  2. THE RULING, and it is decided by one rule: 122.6. Nest of Scarabs asks 'Whenever you put one or more -1/-1 counters on a creature.' Rule 122.6 says that a spell or ability referring to counters being put on an object refers BOTH to putting counters on it while it is on the battlefield AND to an object that is given counters as it enters the battlefield. So 'entering with a -1/-1 counter' is not a separate, exempt category — it is inside the reference. The second half of the question, WHO put it, is answered by 122.6a: the effect may specify a player, and Persist does not, so the object's controller puts those counters on it. Fume Spitter's controller is you (step 1). Therefore YOU put one -1/-1 counter on a creature, and Nest of Scarabs' trigger condition is satisfied. ANSWER: YES, Nest of Scarabs triggers.
    The trigger condition of Nest of Scarabs is met. Note the citation this step deliberately does NOT lean on: sibling stacks 001-005 reach 'you are the actor' through 608.2c, because there the counter is placed by an ability you control following its instructions on a creature already on the battlefield. That reasoning does not by itself reach a permanent entering with counters — no instruction is being followed against an object on the battlefield at that moment. It is 122.6 that brings the entering case inside the phrase, and 122.6a that names you rather than 608.2c. Both roads arrive at the same place here, but only 122.6/122.6a is load-bearing for THIS scenario.
    CR 122.6 — “This refers to putting counters on that object while it’s on the battlefield and also to an object that’s given counters as it enters the battlefield.”
    CR 122.6a — “the effect causing the object to be given counters may specify which player puts those counters on it”
    CR 122.6a — “If the effect doesn’t specify a player, the object’s controller puts those counters on it.”
    CR 110.2a — “If an effect instructs a player to put an object onto the battlefield, that object enters the battlefield under that player’s control unless the effect states otherwise.”
  3. The trigger fires. The game event (you putting one -1/-1 counter on a creature) matches Nest of Scarabs' trigger condition, so the ability triggers automatically at that moment. It does nothing yet and is not yet on the stack.
    One Nest of Scarabs trigger is waiting to be put on the stack. 'That many' is locked to the number of -1/-1 counters in the event: ONE.
    CR 603.2 — “Whenever a game event or game state matches a triggered ability’s trigger event, that ability automatically triggers. The ability doesn’t do anything at this point.”
  4. State-based actions are checked BEFORE the waiting trigger goes on the stack. Fume Spitter is a 1/1 with one -1/-1 counter on it, which subtracts from power and toughness, making it 0/0. A creature with toughness 0 or less is put into its owner's graveyard — Fume Spitter dies immediately, without ever having been available to sacrifice. The check is then repeated; nothing more is performed, and the Nest of Scarabs trigger is put on the stack. Fume Spitter dying does NOT undo the trigger: it had already triggered in step 3, and the trigger is an independent object on the stack that no longer depends on its source event or on Fume Spitter.
    Fume Spitter is in your graveyard. Your battlefield: Nest of Scarabs, three untapped Swamps. Stack: one Nest of Scarabs trigger. This is the pilot's real lesson — Persist on a 1-toughness creature returns it and kills it in the same breath, and what you actually keep is the Insect.
    CR 122.1a — “Similarly, -X/-Y counters subtract from power and toughness.”
    CR 704.5f — “If a creature has toughness 0 or less, it’s put into its owner’s graveyard.”
    CR 117.5 — “Each time a player would get priority, the game first performs all applicable state-based actions as a single event (see rule 704, “State-Based Actions”), then repeats this process until no state-based actions are performed. Then triggered abilities are put on the stack (see rule 603, “Handling Triggered Abilities”).”
    CR 704.3 — “If any state-based actions are performed as a result of a check, the check is repeated; otherwise all triggered abilities that are waiting to be put on the stack are put on the stack, then the check is repeated.”
    CR 603.3 — “Once an ability has triggered, its controller puts it on the stack as an object that’s not a card the next time a player would receive priority.”
  5. You receive priority and pass; every opponent passes in succession (the scenario's stated assumption), so the Nest of Scarabs trigger resolves. It creates that many — one — 1/1 black Insect creature tokens: put one token with those characteristics onto the battlefield under your control. The ability then leaves the stack.
    One 1/1 black Insect creature token enters the battlefield under your control. State-based actions are checked again and none apply (the Insect is 1/1, nothing else is on the battlefield to check). You receive priority in your precombat main phase with the stack empty.
    CR 117.4 — “If all players pass in succession (that is, if all players pass without taking any actions in between passing), the spell or ability on top of the stack resolves”
    CR 701.7a — “To create one or more tokens with certain characteristics, put the specified number of tokens with the specified characteristics onto the battlefield.”
    CR 111.1 — “Some effects put tokens onto the battlefield. A token is a marker used to represent any permanent that isn’t represented by a card.”
    CR 608.2n — “As the final part of an ability’s resolution, the ability is removed from the stack and ceases to exist.”
  6. THE CONTRAST THE QUESTION ASKS FOR: would the answer differ if an effect instead placed a -1/-1 counter on a creature ALREADY on the battlefield? No. The answer is YES in both cases, and 122.6 is precisely the rule that makes them the same — it names both halves in one sentence. What changes is only which rule identifies YOU as the actor. For a creature already on the battlefield, an ability you control instructs you to put the counter and you follow that instruction (608.2c) — that is the route stacks 001-005 take. For a permanent entering with counters, no instruction is being followed against an on-battlefield object, so the actor comes from 122.6a's default: the object's controller. THE ONE PLACE THE TWO GENUINELY DIVERGE, and a pilot should know it: because 122.6a hands the counters to the object's CONTROLLER, a nonlegendary creature returned to the battlefield under an OPPONENT's control with a -1/-1 counter would have that counter put on by that opponent, not by you, and Nest of Scarabs — which says 'whenever YOU put' — would not trigger. In this scenario Fume Spitter enters under your control, so that caveat does not bite.
    Ruling for the deck: Persist IS a legitimate ignition source for Nest of Scarabs — the -1/-1 counter it brings along counts as you putting a counter on a creature, and the Nest pays out one Insect. The goldfish_targets.json listing of Persist as an ignition source is NOT overstated on the rules. The honest caveat is a different one and it is about the BODY, not the trigger: Persist returning a 1-toughness creature (Fume Spitter) yields the Insect and immediately loses the creature to state-based actions, so it ignites without leaving a sacrifice body behind.
    CR 122.6 — “Some spells and abilities refer to counters being put on an object. This refers to putting counters on that object while it’s on the battlefield and also to an object that’s given counters as it enters the battlefield.”
    CR 608.2c — “The controller of the spell or ability follows its instructions in the order written.”
    CR 122.6a — “If the effect doesn’t specify a player, the object’s controller puts those counters on it.”
