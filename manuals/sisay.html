Start with the zone itself. CR 903.4 builds a commander's colour identity from every mana symbol in its mana cost and its rules text, and Sisay's activation — {W}{U}{B}{R}{G} — puts all five colours in hers. A {2}{W} card is your licence to sleeve ninety-nine cards in five colours. That is not a loophole. It is the rule working exactly as printed, and it is the whole reason this deck exists.

The zone's guarantee matters more here than in most decks, because the card it guarantees is a search engine rather than a threat. CR 903.8 gives you that access at a rising price: {2} more for every previous cast from the zone. Sisay runs 3, then 5, then 7, then 9 — the ladder beside this column. Note what the tax does not touch. Her power is recomputed from the battlefield, so a recast Sisay walks back onto a surviving board at full strength. Spot removal costs you the tax and a turn. A sweeper costs you the tax and every rung at once. Those are different injuries, and only the second one is worth insuring against.

The 21-damage clock, CR 903.10a, is on the record and rarely on the plan. At four colours she is a 6/6 and three connections is lethal in theory. In practice her power is a gate rather than a weapon: the deck's actual lethal output is the section called The Kill. Treat commander damage as a bonus the scoreboard occasionally pays.

Now the Coach's half of the page. Protect her; do not budget for recasting her. The simulation holds a protection effect by turn six in 79.8% of games — Shalai, Flowering of the White Tree, Plaza of Heroes' exile mode, and two free instants. A recast costs you more than mana. It costs an activation window at a table that has already seen what she does.

And the political read is fixed before your first land drop. A five-colour commander who tutors on activation gets filed as the archenemy in the pregame, and four Game Changers in the list confirm it out loud. You are not going to argue the table out of that label. You are going to schedule when you deserve it.

THE PLAN

COUNT COLOURS, NOT CARDS

Good Sisay pilots pass with all five pips available and no activation, and it looks like a misplay. The number that decides what she can find is not printed on her card — it is the count of distinct colours sitting beside her.

Five colours, a tutor commander and forty lands: the table has you filed as a greedy pile before you untap. Let them file. What is actually sitting across from them is a fair {2}{W} creature with no tap symbol on her ability and no once-per-turn clause anywhere on the card, in a deck whose only real currency is access.

Read her power, never her mana cost. She is a 2/2 plus one for every colour among your OTHER legendary permanents, and she finds any legendary permanent cheaper than that power. Zero colours is three legal targets. One colour is ten. Two colours is twenty-three. So the move is never "cast Sisay, activate Sisay" — put a colour-dense legend down first and activate second.

Play toward the second colour, then stop climbing at four. One colour to two is thirteen new targets, the largest step on the ladder, and it is the step that first makes Najeela legal. Four colours already opens all 33, because nothing in this list costs more than five. The fifth colour buys combat stats and no access at all — spend that turn holding up protection instead.

Now the bill, because it is where new pilots die. Four cards here demand {W}{U}{B}{R}{G} and exactly one — Jegantha — adds it. Sol Ring makes colourless, Delighted Halfling's coloured mana is for legendary spells only, Unclaimed Territory is creature spells only. Use those three to deploy her a turn early, then pay her with the six that can: Bloom Tender, Faeburrow Elder, Jegantha, Esika, Relic of Legends, Selvala.

Stop mulliganing for the captain. Ten thousand seeded games put her on the battlefield on turn three in 8,028 of them and by turn six in 99 of every 100. The assembly that actually gates you — Sisay plus a multi-colour mana body — arrives by turn six a little over half the time, so keep the fuel and let her go and find the payoffs.

Your removal is a shopping list, not a card in hand. One unconditional creature answer and one sweeper is the whole suite, because Thalia makes their creatures and nonbasic lands enter tapped, Teferi and Kutzil take away the turns they are allowed to act on, and Kambal and Sheoldred bill them for their own spells and draws. Every one of them is legendary at mana value 2 to 4. Go and get the tax that hurts the seat that is beating you, not the scariest one.

Then close with Najeela. She plus Derevi is the one line here that has been walked against the Comprehensive Rules and cleared an adversarial check: an unbounded combat loop that ends the table and costs you nothing in life. It is not free in mana — it re-buys five colours every single combat and needs five of your creatures connecting. Present it on the turn the base can pay that toll, never on the turn the fetch would look impressive.

And know the hole you cannot patch. Twenty-six of your nonland cards are not legendary, so she will never see them: three of the six cards that pay her, every counterspell, both free protection spells and all four Game Changers. She finds payoffs. Fuel you draw.

THE DRILL

MULLIGAN FOR ENABLERS

In a deck whose payoffs fetch themselves, the keep is never about payoffs. The Coach wants fuel and a cheap legend; Ledger's ten thousand hands keep 84.2% of first sevens on the land rule alone — so the judgement was never about lands either.

Keepable first sevens (simulated)84%

KEEP

Command Tower, Temple Garden, Forest, Bloom Tender, Katilda, Dawnhart Prime, Derevi, Empyrial Tactician, Rhystic Study

Unrestricted colour, the best battery piece in the deck, and two bodies carrying five rungs between them. Sisay arrives on curve and the ladder is at three colours by turn five. This is the enabler hand the whole section is asking for.

SHIP

Jodah, the Unifier, Kenrith, the Returned King, Surrak Dragonclaw, Jegantha, the Wellspring, Samut, Voice of Dissent, Plains, Island

Five payoffs, two lands, no fixing. Every legend here is a fetch target — the hand is full of cards Sisay could have found for free, and empty of the fuel she never will.

SHIP

Sol Ring, Delighted Halfling, Unclaimed Territory, Plains, Mountain, Najeela, the Blade-Blossom, Fierce Guardianship

The trap keep. It curves out beautifully and pays nothing: Sol Ring is colourless, the Halfling's coloured mana is legendary-spells-only, the Territory is creature-spells-only. She lands a turn early and stares at a five-pip bill this hand cannot cover.

KEEP

City of Brass, Flooded Strand, Swamp, Arcane Signet, Kutzil, Malamet Exemplar, Esper Sentinel, Path to Exile

Three lands with real colour, a rock that pays any pip, a two-rung legend and one answer to hold. Not flashy — exactly the shape that 84.2% keep rate is made of.

Everyone at this table mulligans hunting for the commander, and she is the single card in the deck you do not need to find — turn-three Sisay in 8,028 games out of 10,000. Keep for the things she cannot go and get.

That is one judgement, and you make it twice a game. Six cards in the 99 can pay her five-pip activation and three of them are not legendary, so those three only ever arrive off the top of your library. Fuel and colours in the opener; payoffs later, from the battlefield, for free.

A keep is three things. Two to four lands including at least one that makes any colour — Command Tower, City of Brass, Mana Confluence, Exotic Orchard, Forbidden Orchard, any fetch, or Secluded Courtyard named on Human. Something that accelerates and pays a coloured pip: Birds of Paradise, Bloom Tender, Faeburrow Elder, Arcane Signet, Fellwar Stone, Relic of Legends. And a cheap multicolour legend, which is what turns a hand that casts her into a hand that climbs.

Katilda, Kinnan, Lotho and Saffi each buy two rungs for two mana; Derevi buys three for three. A seven with one of those and one any-colour source is a snap keep — stop deliberating and shuffle up.

Case A-002 Whether Najeela plus Derevi is genuinely a two-card infinite
Rules version June 19, 2026 · status: cleared in 3 review cycle(s) · tap to open Verified
The question put to the record
You attack with Najeela and Derevi. (a) In the first combat, how many Warriors are declared as attackers, and how many Najeela triggers result? Do the tokens Najeela creates, which enter tapped and attacking, themselves trigger Najeela? (b) How many separate Derevi untap triggers do you get from first-combat damage, and can they untap enough lands to pay Najeela's {W}{U}{B}{R}{G} a second time? (c) Track the number of attacking creatures and the number of Derevi triggers through the second and third combats. Does the loop become self-sustaining, and if so on which combat? (d) Is the claim that these two cards alone form an infinite combo accurate, or does the loop depend on resources outside those two cards? State precisely what is required.
  1. Combat phase 1 begins. You control Najeela, the Blade-Blossom (3/2 Human Warrior) and Derevi, Empyrial Tactician (2/3 Bird Wizard), both untapped and controlled since before this turn, plus five untapped lands. Both opponents are at 40 with empty boards.
    The combat phase runs beginning of combat -> declare attackers -> declare blockers -> combat damage -> end of combat. Nothing is on the stack yet; the first thing that happens is the beginning of combat step.
    CR 506.1 — “The combat phase has five steps, which proceed in order: beginning of combat, declare attackers, declare blockers, combat damage, and end of combat.”
    CR 500.1 — “The beginning, combat, and ending phases are further broken down into steps, which proceed in order.”
  2. COMBAT 1 - beginning of combat step: establish who is being attacked. This is a three-player game, so a defending player must be determined before attackers are declared; the scenario stipulates you attack the first opponent (opponent A).
    Commander's default multiplayer setup is Free-for-All with the attack multiple players option, so unless the table has agreed otherwise that is the configuration in force here. A Free-for-All table must use exactly one of attack left, attack right, or attack multiple players. Under attack multiple players — the Commander default — NO turn-based choice is made in this step: every opponent is already a defending player, and you choose which player each creature attacks individually as you declare it. 506.2a's turn-based choice applies only to the other branch, and even there it is scoped: 506.2a covers the case where the attacking player chooses, and it expressly notes that the choice may instead be DICTATED by the variant or option in use — which is what attack left and attack right do (they hand you a fixed defending player, you do not pick one). So it is wrong to say 'either way the choice is a turn-based action you make'; under attack multiple players there is no such choice, and under attack left/right the choice is not yours. What IS true on every branch is that the determination is made fresh in EVERY combat phase, because each additional combat phase Najeela creates has its own beginning of combat step and its own declare attackers step. Under the Commander default that is what later licenses pointing the army at opponent B (step 22); under attack left/right the redirect in step 22 would NOT be available, because the defending player would be fixed by seating.
    CR 903.2 — “The default multiplayer setup is the Free-for-All variant with the attack multiple players option and without the limited range of influence option.”
    CR 806.2b — “Exactly one of the attack left, attack right, and attack multiple players options must be used.”
    CR 802.2 — “As the combat phase starts, the attacking player doesn’t choose an opponent to become the defending player. Instead, all the attacking player’s opponents are defending players during the combat phase.”
    CR 506.2a — “Unless all the attacking player’s opponents automatically become defending players during the combat phase, the attacking player chooses one of their opponents as a turn-based action during the beginning of combat step. (Note that the choice may be dictated by the variant being played or the options chosen for it.)”
    CR 803.1 — “Some multiplayer games use the optional attack left or attack right rules.”
    CR 506.1 — “The combat phase has five steps, which proceed in order: beginning of combat, declare attackers, declare blockers, combat damage, and end of combat.”
  3. COMBAT 1 - declare attackers. You declare Najeela and Derevi as attackers, choosing opponent A as the player each of them attacks.
    Two creatures are declared as attackers. Exactly ONE of them is a Warrior: Najeela is a Human Warrior; Derevi is a Bird Wizard. Declaring them taps them (neither has vigilance). Both were legal choices: untapped and controlled since the turn began. Each attacker's defending player is chosen here, per creature.
    CR 508.1a — “The active player chooses which creatures that they control, if any, will attack. The chosen creatures must be untapped, they can’t also be battles, and each one must either have haste or have been controlled by the active player continuously since the turn began.”
    CR 802.3 — “As the attacking player declares each attacking creature, they choose a defending player, a planeswalker controlled by a defending player, or a battle protected by a defending player for it to attack.”
    CR 508.1f — “The active player taps the chosen creatures. Tapping a creature when it’s declared as an attacker isn’t a cost; attacking simply causes creatures to become tapped.”
    CR 508.1k — “Each chosen creature still controlled by the active player becomes an attacking creature.”
  4. Najeela's first ability triggers ONCE (not twice) on the attack declaration, and is put on the stack when a player would next receive priority.
    “Whenever a Warrior attacks” triggers per Warrior declared as an attacker. Najeela is the only Warrior declared, so exactly 1 trigger. Derevi (Bird Wizard) is not a Warrior and generates nothing. Trigger count, combat 1 = 1.
    CR 508.3a — “An ability that reads “Whenever [a creature] attacks, . . .” triggers if that creature is declared as an attacker.”
    CR 603.2c — “An ability triggers only once each time its trigger event occurs.”
    CR 603.3 — “Once an ability has triggered, its controller puts it on the stack as an object that’s not a card the next time a player would receive priority.”
  5. The Najeela trigger resolves. You choose “yes,” and its controller (you) creates one 1/1 white Warrior creature token that’s tapped and attacking opponent A.
    Token A is put onto the battlefield already tapped and attacking; since Najeela's ability does not name a defending player, you choose one as it enters — opponent A. Attacking creatures in combat 1 = 3 (Najeela, Derevi, token A). Total power on the table = 3 + 2 + 1 = 6.
    CR 701.7a — “To create one or more tokens with certain characteristics, put the specified number of tokens with the specified characteristics onto the battlefield.”
    CR 111.2 — “The player who creates a token is its owner. The token enters the battlefield under that player’s control.”
    CR 508.4 — “If a creature is put onto the battlefield attacking, its controller chooses which defending player, planeswalker a defending player controls, or battle a defending player protects it’s attacking as it enters the battlefield (unless the effect that put it onto the battlefield specifies what it’s attacking).”
  6. KEY CHECK (question a): token A does NOT trigger Najeela, even though it is a Warrior and is attacking.
    A creature put onto the battlefield attacking was never declared as an attacker, so for trigger purposes it never “attacked.” Najeela's “Whenever a Warrior attacks” therefore does not see it. It IS an attacking creature for every other purpose — it deals combat damage (step 8), it is untapped by Najeela's activated ability (step 12) and it can be declared again next combat (step 15). The tokens simply do not chain within a single combat: 1 Warrior declared -> 1 trigger -> 1 token, full stop. (This is what keeps the loop finite per combat instead of infinite inside combat 1.)
    CR 508.3a — “Such abilities won’t trigger if a creature is put onto the battlefield attacking.”
    CR 508.4 — “Such creatures are “attacking” but, for the purposes of trigger events and effects, they never “attacked.” They remain attacking creatures until they’re removed from combat or the combat phase ends, whichever comes first.”
  7. Declare blockers step: opponent A controls no creatures, so no blocks are declared. All three attackers are unblocked.
    Blocking is a choice the DEFENDING player makes from among creatures they control; opponent A controls no creatures, so there is nothing they can choose and no block can be declared. Every attacker therefore stays unblocked, and each unblocked attacker assigns its combat damage to the player it is attacking. This is the standing assumption for every combat in this line (the scenario stipulates the opponents have no blockers).
    CR 509.1a — “The defending player chooses which creatures they control, if any, will block. The chosen creatures must be untapped and they can’t also be battles.”
    CR 510.1b — “An unblocked creature assigns its combat damage to the player, planeswalker, or battle it’s attacking.”
    CR 510.1a — “Each attacking creature and each blocking creature assigns combat damage equal to its power.”
  8. COMBAT 1 combat damage step: Najeela (3), Derevi (2) and token A (1) deal 6 combat damage to opponent A simultaneously.
    Opponent A: 40 -> 34. No lifelink yet (Najeela's activated ability has not been used), so you gain nothing from this damage.
    CR 510.2 — “Second, all combat damage that’s been assigned is dealt simultaneously. This turn-based action doesn’t use the stack.”
    CR 120.3a — “Damage dealt to a player by a source without infect causes that player to lose that much life.”
    CR 119.2 — “Damage dealt to a player normally causes that player to lose that much life.”
  9. KEY CHECK (question b): Derevi's ability triggers THREE times — once per creature that dealt combat damage to a player — not once for the whole damage event.
    Combat damage is dealt simultaneously, but that single event contains three separate occurrences of “a creature you control deals combat damage to a player,” so the ability triggers once per creature. The three triggers are put on the stack the next time a player would receive priority, each choosing a target permanent (you target your own lands). Derevi trigger count, combat 1 = 3 = number of attacking creatures.
    CR 603.2c — “However, it can trigger repeatedly if one event contains multiple occurrences.”
    CR 603.3 — “Once an ability has triggered, its controller puts it on the stack as an object that’s not a card the next time a player would receive priority.”
    CR 115.1d — “A triggered ability is targeted if it identifies something it will affect by using the phrase “target [something],””
  10. With the three Derevi triggers on the stack you receive priority and activate Najeela's second ability (legal: you are in a combat phase). LINE CHOICE — this resolution takes line L2: you announce the activation FIRST and tap the five lands DURING cost payment, not while holding priority.
    Announcing the ability puts it on the stack; activating an ability then follows the casting steps, and the last of those steps is paying the total cost. A player may activate mana abilities while paying that cost — that is precisely when a mana payment is being asked for — and those mana abilities do not use the stack, so the five mana appear in your pool and the {W}{U}{B}{R}{G} is paid in full. The important consequence of taking line L2 is that you do NOT have priority at the moment the lands are tapped: priority is only handed back after a mana ability when the player HAD priority before activating it, and during cost payment you did not. Najeela's ability is therefore put on the stack ABOVE the three Derevi triggers — but it is NOT the last thing to go on the stack, because the City of Brass trigger produced by the cost payment is still waiting (step 11). Resolution order will be: City of Brass trigger, then Najeela's ability, then the three Derevi triggers. Mana Confluence's “Pay 1 life” is likewise part of the activation cost of its mana ability, not a trigger — it never uses the stack.
    CR 117.1b — “A player may activate an activated ability any time they have priority.”
    CR 602.2b — “The remainder of the process for activating an ability is identical to the process for casting a spell listed in rules 601.2b–i.”
    CR 601.2h — “The player pays the total cost.”
    CR 118.2 — “If a cost includes a mana payment, the player paying the cost has a chance to activate mana abilities.”
    CR 605.3a — “A player may activate an activated mana ability whenever they have priority, whenever they are casting a spell or activating an ability that requires a mana payment, or whenever a rule or effect asks for a mana payment, even if it’s in the middle of casting or resolving a spell or activating or resolving an ability.”
    CR 605.3b — “An activated mana ability doesn’t go on the stack, so it can’t be targeted, countered, or otherwise responded to. Rather, it resolves immediately after it is activated.”
    CR 405.6c — “If a player had priority before a mana ability was activated, that player gets priority after it resolves.”
    CR 405.2 — “Each time an object is put on the stack, it’s put on top of all objects already there.”
  11. City of Brass's triggered ability goes on the stack ON TOP of Najeela's ability. “Whenever this land becomes tapped, it deals 1 damage to you” triggered when you tapped City of Brass during cost payment in step 10.
    This is the L2 bookkeeping, stated explicitly because the two lines order the stack differently. Under L2 you were paying a cost when City of Brass became tapped, so you did not have priority; 117.3c (which returns priority to a player who HAD priority when they activated an ability) applies to your activation of NAJEELA's ability, not to the land taps, and 405.6c does not hand priority back after the mana abilities either. A triggered ability waits and is put on the stack the next time a player would receive priority — here, the moment you receive priority after finishing the activation — and it becomes the topmost object on the stack. Najeela's ability is already on the stack by then, so the City of Brass trigger sits ABOVE it and resolves FIRST, dealing you 1 damage (you: 40 → 39); Najeela's ability resolves next (step 12), then the three Derevi triggers (step 13). (The alternative line L1 — tap the lands while holding priority, float the mana, then activate Najeela — is equally legal but produces the OPPOSITE stack order: the City of Brass trigger would be put on the stack beneath Najeela's ability and Najeela's ability would resolve first. Either way 1 damage is dealt and nothing downstream changes, but this resolution commits to L2 so the step order 11 → 12 → 13 is the true resolution order.) The trigger is NOT a mana ability: it cannot add mana to a mana pool, so it uses the stack like any other trigger. Every tap of City of Brass puts another copy of this trigger on the stack, including the repeat taps inside a single damage step described in step 17. Mana Confluence is the contrast case: its life payment is a cost, so it produces no stack object.

    CR 603.3 — “Once an ability has triggered, its controller puts it on the stack as an object that’s not a card the next time a player would receive priority. See rule 117, “Timing and Priority.” The ability becomes the topmost object on the stack.”
    CR 117.3c — “If a player has priority when they cast a spell, activate an ability, or take a special action, that player receives priority afterward.”
    CR 405.6c — “If a player had priority before a mana ability was activated, that player gets priority after it resolves.”
    CR 405.2 — “Each time an object is put on the stack, it’s put on top of all objects already there.”
    CR 605.1b — “A triggered ability is a mana ability if it meets all of the following criteria: it doesn’t require a target (see rule 115.6), it triggers from the activation or resolution of an activated mana ability (see rule 605.1a) or from mana being added to a player’s mana pool, and it could add mana to a player’s mana pool when it resolves.”
    CR 120.3a — “Damage dealt to a player by a source without infect causes that player to lose that much life.”
    CR 118.1 — “A cost is an action or payment necessary to take another action or to stop another action from taking place.”

    CR 506.4b — “Tapping or untapping a creature that’s already been declared as an attacker or blocker doesn’t remove it from combat and doesn’t prevent its combat damage.”

  17. COMBAT 2 mana check (the break-even point) — THE MECHANISM SPELLED OUT. With the 5 Derevi triggers on the stack, repeat this cycle once per trigger: (i) holding priority, tap a land for mana — a mana ability, so it resolves immediately and the mana sits in your pool; (ii) pass priority; if that tap was City of Brass, its trigger is already on top and resolves first for 1 damage to you, then the next pass resolves the top Derevi trigger, untapping that same land; (iii) you receive priority again the moment it resolves; (iv) tap the now-untapped land again. Mana accumulated this way does not drain between iterations because the pool only empties at end of step.
    Each of the N triggers is therefore worth up to one extra mana within the one damage step, without ever needing more than one land: 5 triggers = up to 5 extra mana = exactly Najeela's {W}{U}{B}{R}{G}. So FROM COMBAT 2 ONWARD THE LOOP PAYS FOR ITSELF with no lands carried in (in fact 3 untapped lands are carried in from combat 1, giving 3 + 5 = 8 available against a 5 cost, leaving 3 untapped again). The interleave is legal because the stack resolves one object at a time and the active player gets priority after each resolution — you are never forced to let the whole stack empty first. Second activation -> untap all attackers, grant trample/lifelink/haste (now including B and C), add combat phase 3.

  21. CRITICAL CHECK: what happens to the 17 creatures attacking opponent A now that opponent A has left the game mid-combat? Answer: nothing. They are NOT removed from combat, so Najeela's activation later in this same damage step still finds 17 attacking creatures to untap and give haste.

The only candidate drains are life: 1 damage per City of Brass tap (a triggered ability that uses the stack, once per tap — step 11) and 1 life per Mana Confluence tap (a cost).
