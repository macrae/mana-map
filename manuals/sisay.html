THE PLAN

COUNT COLOURS, NOT CARDS

Why do good Sisay pilots pass with five untapped lands and no activation? Because the number that decides what she can find is not printed on her card — it is the count of distinct colours sitting next to her.

Everything routes through one number, and it is not Sisay's. Her power is 2 plus the count of distinct colours among your OTHER legendary permanents, and her gate is mana value strictly less than that power. Counted against the 99, the ladder runs 3 legal targets at power 2, 10 at power 3, 23 at power 4, 28 at power 5, and 33 at power 6 - every legendary permanent in the deck. The 2-to-3 step is the largest single jump in the deck, plus thirteen targets from one card, and it is the moment the deck stops being a pile of undersized legends and becomes a toolbox. The ceiling arrives one rung later than you would expect: nothing in this list exceeds mana value 5, so four distinct colours already unlocks everything. The fifth colour buys combat stats and Jodah's anthem and precisely zero new access.

Two facts about her cost shape every turn you play. First, there is no tap symbol anywhere in the ability - summoning sickness never gates her, there is no once-per-turn clause, and the only cap on activations is how many times you can produce {W}{U}{B}{R}{G}. Second, that cost is five coloured pips with zero generic, which means most of what looks like acceleration here cannot pay it: Sol Ring makes {C}{C}, Delighted Halfling's coloured mana is restricted to legendary spells and an activated ability is not a spell, and Unclaimed Territory is creature-spells-only. What does pay are Command Tower, Arcane Signet, Plaza of Heroes' third mode, Secluded Courtyard named on Human, and the six-card pip battery - Bloom Tender, Faeburrow Elder, Jegantha, Esika, Relic of Legends and Selvala. The seeded goldfish (10,000 iterations, seed 42) says Sisay herself is never the problem: mean cast turn 2.96, 99% by turn six. Assembling her alongside a five-colour source is the real curve, and that lands 55.8% of the time by turn six.

What you do with access is buy answers you never have to hold. The deck carries exactly one unconditional creature removal spell and one sweeper; instead it fetches taxes. Kutzil, Malamet Exemplar and Teferi, Time Raveler decide when your opponents are allowed to act at all; Thalia, Heretic Cathar makes their creatures and nonbasic lands arrive tapped; Kambal and Sheoldred convert their own spells and draws into losses. Every one of those is legendary and mana value 2 to 4, so all of them sit inside the pool once the ladder reaches two or three colours. The same is true of the insurance - Shalai, Voice of Plenty, Flowering of the White Tree, and Plaza of Heroes in the mana base - which the goldfish has available 80% of the time by turn six. Ioreth, Samut, Emiel and Seedborn Muse then turn one activation per turn into several by untapping the battery.

The kill is Najeela, the Blade-Blossom, and the manual is only willing to promise you one version of it. The Najeela plus Derevi combat loop is the single line in this deck that has been resolved against the Comprehensive Rules and passed an adversarial check - it is a genuine, deterministic, unbounded combat loop that kills the table, though it is not the self-contained two-card infinite the combo data advertises. Nine other lines the combo data flags for this deck - Najeela with Faeburrow Elder, with Esika, with Selvala, the Emiel blink loops, the Ioreth-Samut-Selvala mana line, the five-card Jegantha line - are unverified here. Play them as board states you may stumble into, not as plans. Note also where the fourth colour usually comes from: among the 33 other legendary permanents, white appears on 19 and green on 14, but blue on only 8, red on 6 and black on 5, and the only red source at mana value 3 or less is Najeela herself. The colour that finishes your ladder and the card that makes you the archenemy are frequently the same card.

The honest weakness is structural and the artifacts make it unmistakable. Twenty-six of the 99 are not legendary and are therefore permanently outside the tutor's reach: three of the six cards that can pay her activation (Bloom Tender, Faeburrow Elder, Relic of Legends), all six counterspells, both free protection instants, and all four Game Changers. Sisay finds payoffs. She does not find her own fuel or her own protection. That is why the bracket floor holds at 4 no matter what happens to the combo lines - the Game Changer count of four is already over bracket 3's cap of three - and it is why the mulligan section below is about enablers rather than about her.

Start with the rule that makes this a different game. CR 903.6: "At the start of the game, each player puts their commander from their deck face up into the command zone." Sisay, Weatherlight Captain is not a card you hope to draw — she is face up on the table before anybody has looked at seven cards, and in a deck built on thirty-three one-of legendary permanents that guarantee is the whole architecture. Singleton is why: CR 903.5b requires every nonbasic card in the 99 to have a different English name, so consistency in this format normally comes from redundancy of effect rather than copies of a card. This deck buys consistency a different way. It runs one repeatable battlefield tutor and treats thirty-three singletons as a sideboard it can reach into, and the tutor is the one card the format promises you every single game. That is why Keep or Ship, four departments from now, asks your opening hand for colours and fuel and never once asks it for a payoff.

Now the bill, and read the ladder beside this file while you do. CR 903.8: a commander cast from the command zone "costs an additional {2} for each previous time the player casting it has cast it from the command zone that game." Sisay is {2}{W}, so the schedule is three mana, then five, then seven, then nine. Put those against the simulation's own numbers: 4.06 mean available mana on turn four, 5.87 on turn six, 8.27 on turn ten. The first cast is trivially on curve — the model has her down by turn three in 8,028 of 10,000 games. The second cast is a five-mana turn that leaves you roughly one mana, which in this deck means a turn with no activation in it. The third is the entire turn and most of the next one.

Here is the distinction good pilots make and bad ones don't. Spot removal aimed at Sisay costs you the tax and the tempo — it does not cost you the ladder, because her power comes from colours among your other legendary permanents and those are still standing when she comes back. A sweeper is the expensive one: it takes the rungs, and Sisay returns to a battlefield where her gate reads "mana value less than 2" and three cards in the deck qualify. Price those two threats differently. Against a pod that answers creatures one at a time, pay the tax and rebuild. Against a pod holding a wrath, keep a two-colour legend in hand rather than on the battlefield, because the hand copy restores two rungs after the wipe and the board copy dies with everything else.

So: protection or speed? The strategy companion is honest about the trade — protection slots and held mana cost you every game, while removal only costs you some — and Cullen's taxonomy splits the options into constant protection, single-use instants, and flexible modal slots. This deck's version of that argument has an unusual shape, because most of its insurance is unfetchable. The simulation has protection available in 88.4% of games and 79.8% by turn six, at a mean of turn 2.3 — but Fierce Guardianship, Flawless Maneuver, Heroic Intervention, Grand Abolisher, Silence and all six counterspells are non-legendary, which means Sisay cannot go and get a single one of them. Exactly four insurance pieces sit inside her range: Shalai, Voice of Plenty, Flowering of the White Tree, Dihada, Binder of Wills, and Plaza of Heroes in the mana base. Our rule: fetch Shalai on the activation before you need her, because she gives hexproof to you and to every other creature you control and cannot protect herself.

Color identity, and the joke this deck is built on. CR 903.4 defines color identity as "the color or colors of any mana symbols in that card's mana cost or rules text," and Sisay's rules text carries {W}{U}{B}{R}{G}. Her identity is therefore all five colours and the 99 is unrestricted — nothing in Magic is locked out of this deck. Then the same five symbols that opened the door become the door's price: her activation is five coloured pips with zero generic, paid over and over. Color identity gives you every card in the format and the activation cost takes most of your mana base back. That is why three of this deck's own accelerants cannot pay her at all — Sol Ring makes {C}{C} against a cost with no generic component, Delighted Halfling's coloured mana is restricted to legendary spells and an activated ability is not a spell, and Unclaimed Territory is creature-spells-only. It is also why Command Tower here is genuinely any colour, and why Plaza of Heroes' third mode, "add one mana of any color among legendary permanents you control," is one of the best lines of text in the deck.

Is the 21-damage clock real? CR 903.10a: "A player who's been dealt 21 or more combat damage by the same commander over the course of the game loses the game." Sisay at three colours is a 5/5, which is five connections; at four colours she is a 6/6, which is four. That is a shorter clock than you would guess and it is still not a plan — you need her untapped to activate, and her ability has no tap symbol precisely so you can do both, which is a bigger deal than the damage. Track the number on one opponent as a tiebreaker, and note the one card that turns her connections into value on purpose: Kutzil, Malamet Exemplar draws you a card whenever a creature with power greater than its base power connects, and a pumped Sisay is always that creature.

The political read is the gentlest thing about this deck and the most useful. A freshly cast Sisay is harmless and experienced players know it — a 2/2 with a gate of "mana value less than 2" is not worth a removal spell. Your grace period ends at the third colour, not at the commander. Sequence the colours onto bodies that read fair, hold the five pips until you intend to spend them, and remember the asymmetry almost nobody else at the table has: your scariest cards live in your library and you can reach them at will, so you never need to hold a finisher in hand. ★ One open question, flagged rather than stamped: whether Sisay may activate on the turn she lands. Her cost contains no tap symbol, which on a plain reading of the summoning-sickness rule means yes — but the case file we wrote to prove it did not clear our checker inside the iteration bound, so it appears in this issue as judgment and not as a verdict. Judge's Desk names it.

Verified line 002

Whether Najeela plus Derevi is genuinely a two-card infinite

The combo data reports Najeela, the Blade-Blossom plus Derevi, Empyrial Tactician as a two-card infinite and uses it as a driver for this deck's bracket 4 floor. This line was resolved step by step against the Comprehensive Rules and re-audited across three checker passes, and the verdict on that claim is partially accurate - which is worth more to you at the table than a yes would have been.

The loop is real. It is deterministic, it is unbounded, and it kills the table. What it is not is self-contained: Najeela and Derevi produce no mana between them, and mana pools empty at every step change, so each iteration's {W}{U}{B}{R}{G} has to be made again from scratch. The arithmetic that decides whether you can do that is the arithmetic of attackers. Untapping the attackers and adding a combat phase means the next combat declares everything again, and every attacker except Derevi is a Warrior who makes one more token, so attackers follow A' = 2A - 1: three, five, nine, seventeen, thirty-three. Derevi's triggers equal the number of creatures connecting, while Najeela's cost stays fixed at five. Combat 1 therefore yields only three triggers and must be pre-funded from your untapped lands; combat 2 breaks exactly even at five; combat 3 runs away with nine. Damage per combat is A + 3 - six, eight, twelve, twenty - so the first defending player dies during combat 4.

The other correction is worth money. The loop's life cost is 0, not 2. Command Tower covers {B} through Sisay's five-colour identity, and Plaza of Heroes' third mode covers W, U, R and G off the colours Najeela and Derevi already put on the battlefield - five taps needing only three Plaza untaps, which combat 1's three Derevi triggers supply exactly. City of Brass and Mana Confluence never have to be tapped at all, because activating a mana ability is never mandatory. The two life per combat you will see quoted is the price of the naive line that taps all five lands; it is not a floor. Practically: both halves are mana value 3, so both come off a Sisay at power 4 - two colours among your other legends - and Derevi is a three-colour card who gets you most of the way there by herself.

The question
You attack with Najeela and Derevi. (a) In the first combat, how many Warriors are declared as attackers, and how many Najeela triggers result? Do the tokens Najeela creates, which enter tapped and attacking, themselves trigger Najeela? (b) How many separate Derevi untap triggers do you get from first-combat damage, and can they untap enough lands to pay Najeela's {W}{U}{B}{R}{G} a second time? (c) Track the number of attacking creatures and the number of Derevi triggers through the second and third combats. Does the loop become self-sustaining, and if so on which combat? (d) Is the claim that these two cards alone form an infinite combo accurate, or does the loop depend on resources outside those two cards? State precisely what is required.

Result. Najeela + Derevi is a genuine, deterministic, unbounded combat loop, but not a self-contained two-card infinite: it needs a mana base that can produce {W}{U}{B}{R}{G} every combat and at least five creatures connecting with a player. Combat 1: 2 attackers declared, 1 Warrior declared, 1 Najeela trigger, 1 token (tokens put onto the battlefield attacking never 'attacked', so they never re-trigger Najeela in the combat that made them); 3 attackers, 6 damage, 3 Derevi triggers -- 2 short of the 5-mana activation, which is instead paid by the 5 pre-combat untapped lands. Combat 2: 3 declared, 2 Warriors, 2 new tokens, 5 attackers, 8 damage, 5 Derevi triggers = exactly the 5-mana cost -> BREAK-EVEN; the loop becomes self-sustaining on combat 2, via tap -> pass priority -> Derevi untap resolves -> retap inside one damage step. Combat 3: 5 declared, 4 Warriors, 4 new tokens, 9 attackers, 12 damage, 9 triggers -> surplus of 4; runaway from here. Attackers follow A' = 2A - 1 (3, 5, 9, 17, 33 ...) and Derevi triggers equal attacker count, while the cost stays fixed at 5, so the margin only widens. Damage per combat = A + 3 (6, 8, 12, 20, 36 ...): opponent A dies as a state-based action during combat 4's damage step, and because a defending player leaving the game is not one of CR 506.4's removal causes the 17 attackers stay in combat, so that combat's Najeela activation still untaps them and adds combat 5. Each added combat phase has its own beginning of combat and declare attackers step, and Commander's default attack multiple players option makes every opponent a defending player, so the army is legally redirected at opponent B: 17 creatures declared in combat 5, 16 Warrior triggers, 33 attacking for 36, and 68 in combat 6, which kills them. The loop's life cost is 0 per combat at the floor — Command Tower plus Plaza of Heroes cover all five colours with three Plaza untaps, so City of Brass and Mana Confluence never need to be tapped.

READING THE POD

NOBODY KILLS A 2/2

You resolve on turn three in 8,028 of 10,000 simulated games and the table lets you — because a fresh Sisay genuinely is harmless. Here is the exact board state where that stops being true, and the removal that actually beats you when it does.

You are on the table's radar from turn three, and the only question is whether they leave you there. Sisay resolves on turn three in 8,028 of 10,000 seeded goldfish iterations (mean 2.96, 99.1% by turn six), so the pod sees your commander before it sees anything else — and at a bracket 4 table where four Game Changers were named in the pregame conversation, nobody extends you the durdly-five-colour-goodstuff benefit of the doubt. What buys you time is that a freshly cast Sisay is genuinely harmless, and good players can see that too: with no other legendary permanent out she is a 2/2 whose gate reads 'mana value less than 2' — three cards in the entire deck — and with zero legendary lands in the list, the mana-value-0 floor that would otherwise always be live simply isn't there. Nobody spends removal on that.

The real threshold is unusually legible, and it isn't Sisay — it's colours among your OTHER legendary permanents. The ladder runs 3 / 10 / 23 / 28 / 33 fetchable targets at power 2 / 3 / 4 / 5 / 6. The 2-to-3 colour step is the whole game: plus thirteen targets in one card, from 'pile of undersized legends' to 'toolbox that finds an answer to almost anything.' The fifth colour is a trap for your attention — it buys combat stats and Jodah's anthem and zero new access, because nothing in this deck costs more than five. Your grace period expires at the third colour. Spend it there.

Four things flip you from ignored to archenemy, and only one of them is a card. First: five untapped colour-producing sources next to an untapped Sisay. Anyone counting mana reads that as 'activation available,' and note that she has no tap symbol at all — summoning sickness never gates her, so 'she only just came down' is not the reprieve opponents assume. Second, and louder than any legend you fetch: an omni-mana body resolving. Bloom Tender, Faeburrow Elder, Jegantha, Esika. The table knows the activation is five distinct pips with zero generic, so the body that solves that is the tell. Third: Najeela, the Blade-Blossom, at which point you are simply the archenemy. Fourth: a second activation inside one turn cycle, which retires any remaining doubt about what the deck is.

So sequence the colours onto bodies that read fair. Katilda (1/1), Reki (1/2), Yoshimaru (1/1), Bristly Bill, Lotho — and prefer the two-colour legends, because Katilda, Kutzil, Kinnan, Lotho, Kambal and Saffi each buy two rungs on one unthreatening card. Hold the five-pip payment until you intend to spend it: visibly open WUBRG taxes the whole table's plays, but it also bluffs interaction you structurally cannot have, since all six counterspells and both free protection instants are non-legendary and must be drawn. A tapped-out board holding a modest permanent is quieter than an open board holding nothing.

The deflection asymmetry here is one almost no other deck gets: your scariest cards live in your library and you can reach them at will. Most decks must draw and hold their finisher; you never need Najeela or Jodah in hand. Play the entire midgame as a fair legends deck and stay one activation from the kill, then time the tell for the turn it wins rather than the turn it impresses. Cheap spotlight-management is fetchable too — Loran of the Third Path's '{T}: You and target opponent each draw a card' is a political card with a body, and Kambal and Sheoldred read as hate aimed at the table's engine seat rather than at anyone in particular. EIGHTY-ONE HANDS, NINETY-NINE CARDS

The busiest artist in this deck painted five cards. The second busiest painted four, and they are all the same land cycle. So what does a deck look like when nobody was collecting anything?

There is no featured artist here in the usual sense, and pretending otherwise would be the kind of small lie this magazine exists to avoid. Eighty-one artists painted the ninety-nine cards counted in this deck, across sixty-six different sets. The leader is Steven Belledin with five cards — five percent of the list — and second place is four.

That number is the story. A five-colour singleton toolbox is the most artistically dispersed thing you can build in Commander: thirty-nine lands drawn from every fixing cycle printed in twenty years, thirty-four legendary permanents pulled one at a time out of whichever set happened to print a two-colour body at mana value two. Nobody bought a box that landed on this deck. It was assembled one card at a time by somebody counting colours, and the art follows the counting.

Belledin's five are worth a look precisely because we are not going to claim they mean anything. Exotic Orchard, Forest, Loran of the Third Path, Mikaeus, the Lunarch and Mox Amber — and three of those five are load-bearing in ways this issue has already explained. Mox Amber and Mikaeus are two of the three cards a cold-start Sisay can legally find at power 2, and Loran is the only artifact and enchantment answer in the deck stapled to a legendary body, which makes it the only one she can fetch. One artist happens to sit on the bottom rung of the ladder and on the deck's single tutorable answer. That is a coincidence, and coincidences are more fun when you say so.

One counting note, because it is the whole reason this department is honest. Ninety-nine cards, one hundred copies: the only card in the deck with a quantity above one is a basic Plains. Nils Hamm painted Bloom Tender and that Plains, which is two cards and three copies — and we count him as a two. Per card, never per copy. In a deck like this one the difference is a single basic land; in a deck with twenty-two of them it is the difference between a fact and a flattering lie.

FOUR SEVENS, ONE QUESTION

The model keeps 84.2% of first sevens on a simple land rule, so the interesting question was never whether to keep — it is whether the hand you kept can ever pay five different pips. Decide on each of these before you read the verdict.

Keepable first sevens (simulated)84%

KEEP — the gold standard

Command Tower, Temple Garden, Forest, Birds of Paradise, Katilda, Dawnhart Prime, Bloom Tender, Jodah, the Unifier

Three lands with an unrestricted source, a one-mana any-colour body, a two-colour legend for the second rung, and the battery piece that pays for the activation. Jodah is uncastable for now and it does not matter — he is a payoff, and payoffs are the part you can fetch later.

KEEP — three rungs on one card

Plaza of Heroes, Hallowed Fountain, Sacred Foundry, Windswept Heath, Arcane Signet, Derevi, Empyrial Tactician, Negate

Four lands, an unrestricted rock, and Derevi — green, white and blue on one mana-value-3 card takes Sisay to 5/5 and the pool from 3 cards to 28 by herself. Negate is the half of the deck you are happy to draw rather than fetch.

SHIP — all payoff, no fuel

Plains, Island, Sol Ring, Delighted Halfling, Jodah, the Unifier, Kenrith, the Returned King, Surrak Dragonclaw

Both accelerants get her out a turn early and neither pays one pip of what she does there — Sol Ring is colourless, Delighted Halfling's mana is for legendary spells and an activated ability is not a spell. Three five-drops behind two lands is a hand of things you were always going to be able to fetch.

SHIP — answers with nothing to answer for

Command Tower, Watery Grave, Polluted Delta, Swan Song, Dispel, Negate, Fierce Guardianship
