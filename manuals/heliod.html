FROM THE EDITOR

LIKED AND UNBEATABLE

Three players thank you for the cards, and mean it. Then, on each of their upkeeps, a piece of cardboard nobody has thought about in years asks how many they are holding.

MANA MAP
From the Editor
Vol. 4 · HELIOD, THE WARPED ECLIPSE

The deck in this issue hands every player at the table free cards, and then bills them for holding the cards.

That is the whole idea, and it is worth an issue because of how strange the seat feels. You spend your turn making three other people richer, and some of them thank you and mean it. Then, on each of their upkeeps — the step nobody looks at — a piece of cardboard asks how many cards they are holding and takes the difference out of their life total. You will not attack anybody. Your damage arrives while you are doing nothing, which is the only time it can arrive, because your mana is spoken for by the counterspell in your hand.

There is also a spectacular one-turn kill in here, and its reputation is the wrong way round. The Counselor has walked that line end to end and it is real. Ledger has the rate at which it turns up, and it is small enough to change what you do on turn four.

You will have a good time here if your favourite thing in this game is being liked and being unbeatable in the same evening, and if you can enjoy a turn where you do nothing on purpose. You will not, if you want your damage to be your own idea.

Coach Brightside opens by telling you the best card in the plan deals no damage. He gets narrowed for it twice, and the second one is the best thing in the department.

Exhibit one. Heliod, the Radiant Dawn is a white card: {2}{W}{W}, a 4/4 God whose entry returns an enchantment from your graveyard. His colour identity is blue and white anyway. CR 903.4 counts every mana symbol anywhere on the card, and the transform ability reads {3}{U/P}. One hybrid Phyrexian pip in an activation cost is why forty cards in this deck are allowed to ask for blue mana.

Now the guarantee, which is the reason the 99 looks like this. Your commander begins the game in the command zone and is the only card you always have (CR 903.4). Every table-draw engine here was chosen because the back face converts opponents' draws into your discount and your flash. The goldfish casts him on a median turn 4 and has him available in 90.2% of games by turn six.

The tax is on the record: {2}{W}{W} the first time, and each recast from the zone costs {2} more than the last (CR 903.8). Four mana, then six, then eight. Heliod also owes a toll nobody prints — every trip home refunds the flip, so you pay {3}{U/P} again, at sorcery speed only, on a later turn.

Coach's ledger on that: insure, do not re-buy. The Phyrexian pip takes two life instead of blue mana, Teferi's Protection buys a whole turn of immunity, and a held Swan Song is cheaper than paying both tolls twice. In this deck protection is the better spend by a wide margin.

Is the 21-damage clock a plan here (CR 903.10a)? No — a coincidence. The back face is a 4/6 that would need six clean connections in a deck whose attack step is ceremonial. Your clocks are Iron Maiden, Ebony Owl Netsuke and a Reservoir ledger.

The political read is worth more than the combat math. The front face registers as a harmless enchantment rebuy, and the flip is your tell: the turn the Warped Eclipse shows up, three opponents learn you have instant-speed range and a discount that grows every time they draw. Spend that information on the turn you choose.

THE PLAN

PUNISHER FIRST, GIFTS SECOND

You are about to hand three opponents free cards, on purpose, every turn. Ten cards in this 99 fill their hands and six charge for the result — the order is the whole skill.

The table has you filed as the nice one. You handed out cards, you never attacked, you said "sure, take one" three times. Then Iron Maiden invoices them for every card you gave away, on their own upkeep, before they get to spend it.

That is the deck, and you should play it in that order. Ten cards make the whole table draw. Six cards charge for the resulting hand. Both halves are on the battlefield in 56.7% of games and 41.2% of games by turn six — so more than half your games are the clock game, and you play toward the clock. Practical version: when you are holding a punisher and a donation engine, land the punisher first or on the same turn. Never after. A Howling Mine that goes down naked is you paying three opponents rent.

Close with Approach of the Second Sun. You reach a win condition in 61.2% of games and 50.5% by turn six, which is the single most reliable ending this deck has — cast it once, let it slot seventh from the top, gain the 7, and set the redraw with Mystical Tutor when you can. Stack 002 walks the second cast and finds it survivable behind held countermagic. So keep one blue up for that turn instead of spending it on the scary-looking spell that isn't going to kill you.

And then the famous one. Hullbreaker Horror plus a cheap rock plus Aetherflux Reservoir is a verified three-opponent kill — stack 006 does the whole ledger — and you will assemble it in 0.7% of games, 2.4% if a tutor helps, 0.3% by turn six. Read that as a gift, not a plan. Do not hold a hand together waiting for it, do not mulligan toward it, and do not tap out hunting the fourth piece. When all four show up on the same turn with Grand Abolisher available, take it. Every other game, you are the punisher deck, and the punisher deck wins by being boring on schedule.

What this does not model

  • The Reservoir kill assembles in 0.7% of games, 2.4% with the tutor counted — and 0.7% is a ceiling rather than a rate. The requirement is satisfied by Sol Ring OR Arcane Signet, and stack 001 walked the one-rock board and found no loop at all.
  • Hullbreaker Horror is one card deep: 7.1% of opening sevens, 9.1% by turn three, and Long-Term Plans is the only search in the 99 that reaches a creature.
  • The third exit is claimed and unpriced. Jace, Wielder of Mysteries turns drawing from an empty library into a win, and nothing here says which turn this deck empties itself — or whether the Jace is on the battlefield when it does.
  • Unsettled Does Heliod, the Warped Eclipse's cost reduction let you choose a larger X on Braingeyser, Stroke of Genius, Prosperity, Skyscribing, Mathemagics, Secure the Wastes, Walking Ballista, Hangarback Walker or Mikaeus, the Lunarch - and if so, is X chosen before or after the reduction applies?
  • Unsettled While Lightning Greaves is equipped to Heliod, can you still target him with Karametra's Blessing or Guardian of Faith's enters trigger, and is moving the Greaves off in response possible at instant speed?
  • Unsettled Can the second Approach of the Second Sun be cast at an opponent's end step under Heliod's flash grant or Leyline of Anticipation, and is Fierce Guardianship's alternative cost live in that window when Heliod himself is the commander on the battlefield?
  • Simulation 10,000 runs of resource development, not of games — no opponent acts in any of them.

4 further questions about this engine are open and unsettled.

THE PANEL

CLEANUP IS STEALING FROM YOU

Sunny opens on the card in the punisher plan that deals zero damage. Vera narrows him twice, and the second narrowing changes which turn you are allowed to ring the Bell.

Hot take
Cleanup is stealing from you, not from him. He trims to seven and the damage he owed you goes to the graveyard with the cards. The best card in your punisher plan deals zero damage — and it is not Iron Maiden, and it is certainly not Forced Fruition, the one that hands somebody seven cards at once. It is The Second Doctor, or it is Folio of Fancies. Both read "Players have no maximum hand size," and neither has ever burned anybody for a point in its life. Without one of those two on the battlefield, the eighth card you gift a player gets shaved off before you can charge him for it. You can hand a man seventy cards. Iron Maiden still asks him for three.
★ COACHING Coach Sunny Brightside
Counselor Vera Dictum

THE AUDIT

BLUE DOES 79% OF THE WORK

Blue pays 79.2% of this deck's pips and owns 63.4% of its sources. On curve it lands 74.2% of the time off lands alone and 83.9% once the rocks tap, and exactly one card in the 99 feels the difference.

Thirty-three lands across eighteen entries, and only two of them always enter tapped. Seven can enter tapped at all, and five of those are conditional — a shock you can pay through, or a dual that untaps itself once you control two other lands. Against a template that asks for 37-39, thirty-three is under, and the audit says so.

The land count is not the whole supply. Six rocks take it to 39 mana sources against a 37 target, so the deck is short on lands and at target on mana — and that is before a ritual and three cost-reducers that shrink the bills rather than pay them. In the goldfish, the turn-three land drop hits 86.6% of the time and the turn-six one only 47.1%, while mean available mana still climbs from 3.311 on turn three to 5.325 on turn six. The rocks do the second half of that job.

Blue is 79.2% of the pips and 63.4% of the sources; white is 20.8% of the pips and 36.6% of the sources. That is 61 blue pips spread over 40 cards against 16 white pips over 14 — the deck is far bluer than its mana base is, and white is comfortably over-served. Blue lands at 26, lifting to 31 with rocks, against a 36 yardstick; white at 15, lifting to 20, against 22.

On-curve, that reads as 74.2% blue and 78.7% white off lands alone, and 83.9% and 88.1% once the rocks are counted. The 90% figure is a yardstick, not a possibility at thirty-three lands.   • Mana rocks ('{T}: Add') contribute from the turn after they are cast.

Measured on edgar-vampires: 1.264 extra cards by turn ten with both flags against 1.106 with draw alone. A-002 Approach of the Second Sun, second cast off Mystical Tutor ✓ cleared, ? cycle(s) · 8 steps · 20 citations
Rules version June 19, 2026
The question put to the record
Resolve this second cast of Approach of the Second Sun: exactly when is the 'you win the game' condition checked, does the first casting still count toward it even though the card was put back into the library, and at what point can an opponent's counterspell or other interaction still stop the win? If it resolves, state precisely how the game ends.
  1. You have cast Approach of the Second Sun from your hand for {6}{W}. It is the only object on the stack. The casting is already complete, so the spell 'was cast from your hand' for the purpose of its own first sentence, and you receive priority.
    Approach of the Second Sun is a sorcery spell on the stack, cast from your hand. It has no targets, so there is no targeting legality to check on resolution. You hold priority with {U} still available (Swan Song in hand).
    CR 601.2i — “Once the steps described in 601.2a–h are completed, effects that modify the characteristics of the spell as it’s cast are applied, then the spell becomes cast.”
    CR 117.3c — “If a player has priority when they cast a spell, activate an ability, or take a special action, that player receives priority afterward.”
  2. Before Approach can resolve, every player must pass priority in succession. This is the opponents' window — their only window — to interact with the win.
    The opponent known to hold an instant may cast it in response while Approach is on the stack. Anything cast in response goes on top of the stack and resolves before Approach does. If instead all players pass without acting, Approach will resolve (step 5).
    CR 117.4 — “If all players pass in succession (that is, if all players pass without taking any actions in between passing), the spell or ability on top of the stack resolves”
    CR 117.7 — “the new spell or ability has been cast or activated”
    CR 117.7 — “The new spell or ability will resolve first.”
  3. Contingency: the opponent's instant is a counterspell targeting Approach of the Second Sun, and it resolves.
    If that happens, Approach is removed from the stack, it does not resolve, and none of its effects occur — no win-condition check, no life gain, and it is put into your graveyard rather than back into your library. The win is fully stoppable at this point.
    CR 701.6a — “To counter a spell or ability means to cancel it, removing it from the stack. It doesn’t resolve and none of its effects occur. A countered spell is put into its owner’s graveyard.”
  4. Your answer: while the opponent's instant is on the stack, you respond with Swan Song for {U}, targeting that instant ('Counter target enchantment, instant, or sorcery spell. Its controller creates a 2/2 blue Bird creature token with flying.').
    Swan Song goes on top of the stack and resolves first. If it resolves, the opponent's instant is countered and that opponent creates a 2/2 blue Bird creature token with flying; Approach then remains on the stack and resolves as in step 5 (unless someone has further interaction). Honest limits: Swan Song can only counter an enchantment, instant, or sorcery SPELL — if the opponent interacts with an activated or triggered ability instead of a spell, Swan Song cannot stop it; and Swan Song itself can be responded to.
    CR 117.7 — “The new spell or ability will resolve first.”
    CR 701.6a — “To counter a spell or ability means to cancel it, removing it from the stack.”
    CR 113.9 — “Activated and triggered abilities on the stack aren’t spells, and therefore can’t be countered by anything that counters only spells.”
  5. All players pass priority in succession. Approach of the Second Sun begins to resolve, and you follow its instructions in the order written.
    Once resolution begins, no player has priority and nobody can respond any longer — the interaction window of steps 2–4 has closed. The first instruction to evaluate is the conditional first sentence of the card.
    CR 608.1 — “Each time all players pass in succession, the spell or ability on top of the stack resolves.”
    CR 608.2c — “The controller of the spell or ability follows its instructions in the order written.”
    CR 117.2e — “Even if a player is doing so, no player has priority while a spell or ability is resolving.”
  6. The win condition is checked NOW — during resolution, as a resolving spell effect. Both halves of the condition are evaluated: (a) 'this spell was cast from your hand' — true; (b) 'you've cast another spell named Approach of the Second Sun this game' — true, per the game history.
    This is an effect that states a player wins the game (CR 104.2b) — it is not a state-based action and does not wait for any check or priority pass. The moment this instruction is followed, you win. Because the condition is met, the 'Otherwise' clause (put it seventh from the top of your library and gain 7 life) is never performed.
    CR 608.2c — “The controller of the spell or ability follows its instructions in the order written.”
    CR 104.2b — “An effect may state that a player wins the game.”
  7. Why the first casting still counts even though the card went back into the library: the condition asks whether YOU have CAST another spell with that name this game — it checks an event in the game's history, not a property of any current card.
    Casting is an action the player completed earlier this game (the spell 'becomes cast' when the casting steps finish). The physical card later becoming a new object as it was shuffled back into your library changes the card object's identity, but the condition never looks at that object — your casting of it remains a fact of game history. (For the same reason, even a first Approach that had been COUNTERED would satisfy this clause: it was still cast.)
    CR 701.5a — “To cast a spell is to take it from the zone it’s in (usually the hand), put it on the stack, and pay its costs, so that it will eventually resolve and have its effect.”
    CR 601.2i — “Once the steps described in 601.2a–h are completed, effects that modify the characteristics of the spell as it’s cast are applied, then the spell becomes cast.”
    CR 400.7 — “An object that moves from one zone to another becomes a new object with no memory of, or relation to, its previous existence.”
  8. The game ends. You win, immediately, mid-resolution.
    The game ends immediately when a player wins — there are no further priority passes and no state-based-action checks (those only happen when a player would get priority, which never occurs again). Both opponents' life totals (25 and 31) are irrelevant; they do not 'lose to an SBA' — the game simply ends with you as the winner. Note: the limited range of influence option (under which a win instead makes opponents in range lose) applies only to games using that option; Commander does not, so the whole game ends.
    CR 104.1 — “A game ends immediately when a player wins, when the game is a draw, or when the game is restarted.”
    CR 117.5 — “Each time a player would get priority, the game first performs all applicable state-based actions as a single event”
    CR 104.3h — “In a multiplayer game using the limited range of influence option (see rule 801), an effect that states that a player wins the game instead causes all of that player’s opponents within the player’s range of influence to lose the game.”
