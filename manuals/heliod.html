THE PLAN

THE FRIENDLIEST CRIME

Why would a control deck hand three opponents a free Howling Mine? Because in this 99 their draws pay your bills — and feed a meter that reads hands, not life totals.

You're running the friendliest crime in the format: hand the whole table a firehose of cards, then charge for the water. Howling Mine, Font of Mythos, Temple Bell, Dictate of Kruphix, Kami of the Crescent Moon, Folio of Fancies, and the big one — Forced Fruition — all read symmetric, and Kwain and The Second Doctor make the generosity look sincere. Heliod is the hinge. The front face rebuys an enchantment on arrival; the {3}{U/P} flip into the Warped Eclipse grants everything flash and discounts your spells {1} for each card your opponents drew this turn. Every card the table accepts pays your mana bill on the turn you want to act.

The numbers back the patience. The goldfish sim has Heliod down by turn 6 in 90.2% of games (median turn 4). While the pieces gather, you play draw-go behind a counter suite held by turn 6 in 67.2% of games, with Supreme Verdict as the uncounterable reset your own planeswalkers survive.

Your primary win is verified end to end, and it is a storm kill: Hullbreaker Horror + Sol Ring + Arcane Signet + Aetherflux Reservoir (stack 006, checker-passed). The loop — each rock's cast bounces the other, tapped for its mana before it leaves — is exactly mana-neutral (stack 004), so casts are unbounded in one main phase. The Reservoir converts the count into life: cast n nets +n, so k casts pay out k(k+1)/2. From 32 life you need that sum to reach 119, which makes 15 casts the proven minimum — 14 casts pay only 105 and fail.

The verified schedule reads like a heist ledger. Casts 1–6 lift you to 53; the first 'Pay 50 life: 50 damage' activation drops you to 3 and kills the 26-life opponent via state-based actions. Casts 7–12 rebuild to 60; the second activation (60 to 10) kills the 40-life seat. Casts 13–15 reach 52; the third (52 to 2) takes the last, and with every opponent gone you win on the spot — one main phase, ending at 2 life.

The same artifact names the weakness, so respect it. Every activation and every loop cast opens a fully open priority window: activating an ability is not casting a spell, so Hullbreaker never triggers in those windows, and the sorcery-speed loop pieces can't respond to anything. Grand Abolisher is the named answer — during your turn, opponents can't cast spells or activate artifact, creature, or enchantment abilities. He closes exactly the windows the kill opens. Win-condition access is drawn by turn 6 in 50.5% of goldfish runs (61.2% of games ever), and the whole line needs {2} and the pieces, nothing more.

Path two is the clean one: Approach of the Second Sun. The first cast gains 7 life — 14 with Alhammarret's Archive out — and buries the card seventh from the top, trivially shallow when everyone draws in bulk and two doublers accelerate your digging. Mystical Tutor or Long-Term Plans puts it back on top directly. Stack 002 verified the second cast end to end: the win is checked during resolution, as a spell effect, and the first casting still counts because the game tracks the casting event, not the shuffled-away card. Their one and only window is while the second Approach sits on the stack — commit behind held countermagic, and once resolution begins, nothing stops it.

Path three is attrition, and it runs while you sleep. Iron Maiden and Viseling deal upkeep damage equal to hand size minus 4; Ebony Owl Netsuke deals a flat 4 at seven-plus cards. Stack 003 verified the arithmetic: Forced Fruition fills a hand, cleanup trims it to exactly 7, and next upkeep the Owl hits for 4 and the Maiden for 3 — 7 damage without you tapping a land, running on all three opponents' upkeeps. The Second Doctor makes 7 the floor, not the ceiling: no maximum hand size means the trim never happens, so a ten-card hand takes 6 from the Maiden while the Owl keeps firing. The engine-plus-punisher two-piece assembles by turn 6 in 41.2% of games, so most games open as pure control — the deck is built to be great at exactly that — and the moment the four kill pieces line up behind an Abolisher, you stop playing fair.

Ledger's confession: the sim keeps every 2-5-land hand, so it keeps this one. You're smarter than the sim — five lands, two seven-drops, and nothing to do before turn 5. The base is clean — this flood is the hand's fault, not the deck's.

The sim keeps 73.8% of first sevens and mulligans gently — 0.33 per game — so the bar is simple. Two to four lands, one card-flow piece, and something that says no. Kept hands center on 2-3 lands plus rocks: at 33 lands your drops take care of themselves, so the rocks are buying turns rather than covering a shortfall, and Ancient Tomb plus a two-drop rock is how 27.7% of games cast Heliod by turn 3.

You're mulliganing toward flow plus interaction, not toward any combo. A table-draw engine is drawn by turn 6 in 75.4% of games (mean turn 2.5) — call it the 75% promise. Cheap or free interaction is held by then in 67.2% (78.1% of games ever). Those are your reliable axes, and Kwain and Jace Beleren both count among the cheap engines worth keeping a hand for.

The trap has a name: the two-piece. Engine PLUS punisher assembles by turn 6 in only 41.2% of games (56.7% ever, mean turn 4.2), so never keep a marginal hand because it 'promises the lock.' The punishers are inert on their own — Iron Maiden reads hand minus 4, and against a normal hand that's one damage. A hand of two punishers and no draw engine is a mulligan in costume. Ship it.

The same discipline applies doubly to the verified kill: four specific pieces is not a mulligan target. It's what Long-Term Plans and the 75.4% draw engine find for you in the midgame. And don't dig for wincons at hand-quality prices — access is 50.5% by turn 6 with Jace, Wielder of Mysteries counting as a standing wincon, and the tutors carry the rest.

Heliod himself needs no help: 90.2% of games cast him by turn 6, median turn 4. Keep hands that use the turns before him — Remora into rock into Kami (or Kwain) is the curve this deck was built to play.

Turn 6: does Forced Fruition come down against a spellslinger?

The board

You

Battlefield
Turn 6, precombat main. Battlefield: Howling Mine, Iron Maiden, six lands (WWUU + 2 colorless available). Hand: Forced Fruition, Ebony Owl Netsuke, Swan Song, Island. Heliod not yet cast (goldfish mean cast 4.133 — you spent those turns on engine pieces).

The table

Player 2 (Izzet spellslinger) has a storm-adjacent board — two cost reducers, a cantrip engine — and six cards in hand off your Howling Mine. Player 3 (Selesnya go-wide) has eight tokens and swings elsewhere this turn. Player 4 (mono-black reanimator) just tutored and is visibly one untap from something big.
Forced Fruition punishes every opponent spell with seven fresh cards. Against a spellslinger who WANTS cards and a reanimator about to combo, do you deploy it now to feed Iron Maiden and Ebony Owl, hold it and deploy Ebony Owl alone, or hold everything and keep Swan Song up?

Deploy Forced Fruition now and start the punisher clock

The line
Play the Island for seven mana (WWUUU + 2 colorless). Cast Forced Fruition — {U}{U} plus WWCC for the generic — and hold the last {U} for Swan Song. Owl stays in hand until next turn. From here every opponent cast hands its caster seven cards; whoever ends their turn hoarding trims to seven at cleanup and takes Iron Maiden damage each upkeep. Once Owl lands, that is exactly 7 per upkeep at a seven-card hand — the number stack 003 verifies.
Signals sent
Forced Fruition is this deck's loudest tell. A six-mana 'every spell you cast is an event' enchantment reads as the group-slug engine coming online, and the table's assessment updates instantly. You just announced yourself as the archenemy with two artifacts, no Heliod, and one mana held.
Coalition risk
High, and paid for nothing yet. Your asymmetry layer is missing — no Heliod discount, no Tithe or Sphinx on board — so this is the scary half of the engine deployed before the half that makes it yours. Worse, both dangerous seats profit: the spellslinger's cost reducers and cantrip engine turn 'seven cards per spell' into an explosive refill loop, and the reanimator's cleanup trim is a free discard outlet — rule 514.1 lets them choose which cards hit the graveyard, and they will choose the fattest thing they own. Expect the go-wide player to be told, correctly, that you are now the problem.

Know what Fruition is before Heliod and Owl are down: a donation, not a tax. Iron Maiden punishes hand-hoarders, and a spellslinger is the one archetype that never hoards — they metabolize the seven cards into more spells. Stack 003's math is real: the trigger resolves before removal, cleanup trims to seven, Owl 4 + Maiden 3 = 7 per upkeep. But every number in it assumes an opponent who holds cards, and the two seats that scare you tonight are the two that won't.

With Fruition down, even your own interaction donates — the cast trigger hits the stack immediately, so the reanimation spell you Swan Song still draws its caster seven on the way to being countered. The pivot-window rule names this exactly: your win attempt comes when it can't be profitably answered, and a naked Fruition on turn 6 into a tutoring reanimator is the opposite — three players' worth of incentive to remove one enchantment. The one honest case for this line is a table of hoarding, spell-shy opponents. Look around: that is not this table.

Hold Fruition, deploy Ebony Owl Netsuke, keep Swan Song up

The line
Play the Island and cast Ebony Owl Netsuke off the two colorless. Then take the line the mana is begging for: Heliod's front face ({2}{W}{W}) with WWUU, holding the last {U} for Swan Song. Owl + Heliod + counter held — seven mana spent to the point. The spellslinger sits at six cards off your Howling Mine; their own Mine draw puts them at seven-plus by their upkeep unless they dump. Owl's intervening 'if' checks seven or more, and at exactly seven the verified split is Owl 4 + Iron Maiden (7 − 4) = 3 — stack 003's 7-per-upkeep clock, running without donating anyone a single card.
Signals sent
A two-mana trinket and your commander's harmless front face read as a quiet development turn — the table's attention stays on the tutoring reanimator and the eight-token board. And one blue open, from a deck that has shown counterspells, makes three players respect interaction only one of them will test.
Coalition risk
Low. The punisher damage arrives as upkeep triggers, not an announced haymaker — the spellslinger who takes 7 for hoarding blames their own hand. Nobody unites against an Owl. Your deflection budget stays intact for the turn Fruition actually wins something.
Read

You're in the fortunate two-in-five — the core two-piece assembles by turn 6 in only 41.2% of goldfish games — and the discipline that rewards it is sequencing: punishers before the big draw engine, so every refill arrives taxed instead of gifted. Howling Mine is already feeding hands; Owl turns those fed hands into a clock tonight, against exactly the seats that hold cards. Heliod means the {3}{U/P} transform next turn, and the Warped Eclipse is what converts a later Fruition from donation into engine — flash deployment, spells discounted by every card the table draws.

Swan Song stays home for the reanimation spell itself. It counters instants, sorceries, and enchantments — Reanimate and Animate Dead lines both die to it; a creature-land or activated-ability line does not, so watch what the tutor was for. That is the answer-economy bar: the counter is for the card that ends the game, and the reanimator just told you it's coming. Time Fruition for the turn it wins — Eclipse online, punishers established, ideally the spellslinger's library already thin — not the turn it impresses.

Hold everything and keep Swan Song up

The line
Play the Island, cast nothing, pass with seven mana and Swan Song ready. Pure draw-go: wait out the reanimator's turn, wait out the sweeper you haven't seen, deploy nothing until the coast is clear.
Signals sent
Passing with seven up looks like maximum interaction — but this deck holds one Swan Song, not seven counterspells, and the table already sees Howling Mine and Iron Maiden. The quiet-artifact posture stopped fooling anyone the turn Iron Maiden hit the table.
Coalition risk
Minimal optics risk, real tempo cost. You defer to the reanimator's schedule and the spellslinger's engine while your own clock never starts.
Read

This line confuses holding an answer with holding everything. Swan Song costs {U}; the other six mana protect nothing, because until Heliod transforms this deck has no flash — those six mana are dead air, not held interaction. Your claim on this game is inevitability: the punishers are a clock that advances during draw-go, and whoever owns the long game gets to wait. But inevitability has to be running to be owned — an Owl in hand deals zero per upkeep, and a Heliod in the command zone discounts nothing.

You are already behind the commander curve (goldfish mean cast 4.133, 90.2% by turn 6 — this game is in the slow tail), and every fully passive turn is a free draw step for a spellslinger you are feeding with your own Howling Mine. Branch two holds the exact same Swan Song for the exact same reanimation spell and starts a verified 7-damage clock with the spare mana. Choose this line only on concrete evidence of artifact mass removal in a specific hand — evidence this scenario doesn't grant you.

Our call: Hold Fruition, deploy Ebony Owl Netsuke, keep Swan Song up
Every fact in this scenario points the same way, and it's the winning way. Stack 003's verified math — Owl 4 + Maiden 3 = 7 at a seven-card hand — needs opponents who hold cards, and Howling Mine is already pushing the spellslinger there without Fruition's help: the 7-per-upkeep clock starts tonight for two mana and zero donation. Fruition into this table feeds the two seats that punish it worst — a spellslinger who metabolizes seven-card gifts into more spells, and a reanimator for whom the cleanup trim (rule 514.1, walked in 003) is a free, self-selected discard outlet one untap before their big turn. The Owl line also fits Heliod and Swan Song into the same seven mana: the commander progresses toward the Warped Eclipse flip that makes a later Fruition asymmetric, and the counter stays held for the reanimation spell per the answer-economy bar. Deploy the tax before the gift, keep the tell in hand until it wins, and let the strategic frame's warning stand: against spellslinger pods, Forced Fruition is a liability until your asymmetry layer is online.

The flip: when does Heliod become the Warped Eclipse?

The board

You

Battlefield
Turn 7, precombat main. Battlefield: Heliod, the Radiant Dawn (front face), Temple Bell, Font of Mythos, Psychosis Crawler, seven lands ({W}{W}{U}{U} + 3 colorless available). Hand: Counterspell, Approach of the Second Sun, Plains. Life 34.

The table

Everyone is drawing two extra per turn off your engines. Player 2 (Azorius control) holds five cards and open mana. Player 3 (gruul aggro) is at 21 after trading with Player 4. Player 4 (Dimir value) quietly assembled seven cards in hand and has been polite about your engines so far.
Transforming Heliod costs {3}{U/P} at sorcery speed — this turn it competes with holding Counterspell up. The back face gives your spells flash and a discount per card opponents drew this turn, which your own engines feed every turn cycle. Do you flip now (telegraphing the control turn and marking yourself the threat), hold the flip until the turn you intend to cast Approach with counter backup, or never flip and stay the friendly hug deck until the kill?

Flip now — pay the {U/P} with 2 life and keep Counterspell fully up

The line
Play the Plains for eight mana ({W}{W}{W}{U}{U} plus three colorless). Activate the transform for {3}{U/P}, paying the three colorless and 2 life for the Phyrexian pip (34 to 32) — the front face's own reminder text, '{U/P} can be paid with either {U} or 2 life,' is a sequencing lever, not flavor. Pass with Counterspell live ({U}{U}) and {W}{W}{W} spare. Next turn cycle, pick the turn of the opponent who acts right before you: their draw step gives them three cards (one regular plus two from Font of Mythos — 'that player draws two additional cards'), then at their end step ring Temple Bell ('Each player draws a card'), pushing opponents' draws this turn to six — your own Bell draw drains each opponent 1 via Psychosis Crawler ('Whenever you draw a card, each opponent loses 1 life'). With the back face granting 'You may cast spells as though they had flash,' cast Approach of the Second Sun right there for {W} — {6}{W} minus six. It resolves: gain 7, Approach goes seventh from the top, and at three cards per draw step you rebuy it in about two turns for the winning second cast, whose only interaction window is mapped and survivable behind held countermagic in verified stack 002.
Signals sent
The flip is a known tell — an experienced table reads the Warped Eclipse as 'flash everything plus a growing discount,' and Player 2 must respect your end steps from here on. But the flip turn itself presents zero board impact: no attack, no spell resolved, mana still up. You have separated the flip tell from the Approach tell, so no single turn shows both.
Coalition risk
Medium. Player 2 books you as the real control opponent and starts holding counters across your windows, and Player 4's seven-card hand keeps growing off your engines. But the table's live conflict is Player 3 versus Player 4, and the flip offers nobody a profitable answer: Counterspell-style effects hit spells, the transform is an activated ability — not a card on the stack — and any removal spell aimed at Heliod in response walks into your held {U}{U}.
Read

Flip at the first sorcery window where you can shield the aftermath — and this is it. The timing restriction is the whole argument: the transform only ever happens under sorcery timing rules, so it is the one move in this deck you can never make reactively. Wait a cycle, and a removal spell — or the sweeper Player 3's board is begging out of someone — deletes the option and adds recast tax on top. The Phyrexian pip is worth exactly one live Counterspell: pay {U} instead and you keep only a single blue up. Two life out of 34 buys the counter, and the first Approach pays 7 back.

The back face is the deck's entire mana plan — the curve keeps just eleven cards at five-plus mana because Heliod's discount is expected to pay the top end, while Font of Mythos donates two cards per opponent per turn whether you flip or not. Every unflipped turn is donation with no rebate. The flash the back face grants is also what assembles the deck's other verified exit — the Aetherflux Reservoir table-kill (stack 006) — on your own terms, with Grand Abolisher deployable at instant speed as its shield. Flip on the quiet turn: spend deflection budget on the tell that doesn't win, so the budget is still there for the one that does.

Hold the flip; take one big turn later — flip, ring the Bell, Approach with backup

The line
Play the Plains and pass with eight mana up and Counterspell live — the picture of a draw-go turn. On a later turn with nine-plus lands, flip at your main phase ({3} plus 2 life), tap Temple Bell so your three opponents each draw (your spells now cost {3} less), and cast Approach of the Second Sun for {3}{W} in the same main phase, aiming to keep {U}{U} open through its resolution.
Signals sent
Until the commit turn you are the harmless hug deck with open mana — maximum deflection. The commit turn itself is the loudest possible sequence: the commander flips, the Bell rings, and a seven-mana sorcery hits the stack in one motion. Every player at the table re-evaluates you simultaneously.
Coalition risk
Low now, maximal on the commit turn. Stacking both tells into one turn hands Player 2 a single clean decision point with five cards in hand. And check the arithmetic: on your own turn only the Bell feeds the discount — your Font of Mythos draws are yours, not your opponents' — so flip plus Bell plus a {3}{W} Approach costs seven of your mana. At nine lands you hold {U}{U}; at eight you don't, and the Approach crosses the stack unprotected.
Read

This line respects the right instinct — 'hold the protection spell for the turn you commit, not after' — but points it at the wrong cast. The first Approach doesn't win anything: it gains 7 life and sets the clock ('put Approach of the Second Sun into its owner's library seventh from the top and you gain 7 life'). The cast that must survive is the second one, and verified stack 002 already maps that window with countermagic held.

Meanwhile the waiting cost is concrete. The flip is sorcery-only, so one removal spell or wrath during the cycle you spend posing ends the plan entirely, and Font of Mythos donates six opponent cards per full cycle with no discount flowing back. The discount math is real — cost reductions come off the total cost, and a Bell-fed Approach at {3}{W} is legitimate — but the same math is better on an opponent's end step after the flip is already old news. Count Player 2's five cards the way the pivot rule demands: the count argues for flipping on the turn nothing is at stake, not the turn everything is.

Never flip — stay the friendly hug deck and win at sorcery speed

The line
Keep the front face for good. Play the Plains, pass with Counterspell up, and plan to cast Approach of the Second Sun on a future main phase for the full {6}{W}, trusting the table's goodwill and the Psychosis Crawler chip damage to carry the long game.
Signals sent
You stay the table's favorite player right up until you cast a seven-mana sorcery with your shields down. An unflipped Heliod is a forgettable 4/4; nobody re-evaluates you until the Approach hits the stack — and then everyone does at once, with priority in hand.
Coalition risk
Lowest until the cast, then total. At sorcery speed the Approach goes on the stack in your own main phase, giving every opponent the exact priority window verified stack 002 identifies as the only place the win can be stopped — and Player 2 has been banking five cards for precisely this moment.
Read

Run the mana honestly and this line collapses. A noninstant spell is castable only in your main phase with the stack empty, so the unflipped Approach is always a telegraphed main-phase play — and protecting it means paying for Approach plus Counterspell in one breath: nine mana. Goldfish says your mean available mana is 6.2 on turn 8, 6.6 on turn 9, 7.0 on turn 10. The protected sorcery-speed Approach never arrives.

The flip is the deck's hinge precisely because it closes that gap: opponents' draws become your discount and your action moves to their end steps. Refusing it to dodge hypothetical removal is the textbook scared line — you are handing a control deck free draw steps, two extra per turn off your own Font of Mythos, while declining the half of the engine that makes the table pay for them. There are tables where the front face is right: punisher boards that win without Approach, removal-saturated pods where the flip eats an instant kill. This board has no punishers and your only clock is a Crawler — fear of becoming the threat is not a plan, and the table is already eating off you either way.

Our call: Flip now — pay the {U/P} with 2 life and keep Counterspell fully up
Every clock in this scenario ticks against the front face — so flip, and take charge of the game you're already feeding. The transform is the one action this deck can never take reactively (sorcery timing only), and its risk compounds each cycle it waits: Player 2 holds five cards, Player 3's board is pulling a sweeper out of somebody, and an answered Heliod means commander tax plus another sorcery window you may never get. Flipping now costs nothing that matters: the Phyrexian pip converts two of your 34 life into a live Counterspell, the flip itself offers no profitable answer because it is an ability rather than a spell, and the turn shows no other action — far cheaper in deflection budget than any stacked commit turn. What it buys is the whole deck: from the next turn cycle your engines' donations become your discount — the last opponent's Font draws plus one Bell ring make Approach cost {W} at their end step with Counterspell up — and the rebought Approach wins through the exact interaction window verified in stack 002, counter backup already in place. Goldfish closes the case: the unflipped, protected Approach needs nine mana, and mean available mana is only 7.0 on turn 10. Flip on the quiet turn; save the scary card for the turn it wins.

THE TABLE

GENEROSITY, WITH INTEREST

Sixty-one cards in the box argued for ten seats; seven made it, three more were scouted from the wider pool. What survived feeds the proven machines — Mana Vault to flip the loop's sign, Teferi, Time Raveler to back the shield.

The Short List is ten names — seven from the 61-card bench, three scouted from the wider pool — and the pruning did the real work. Spare lands, one-shot cantrip bodies, fair stunners, and token boards that walk into your own Supreme Verdict all fail the same test: an engine deck at a tier-4 table doesn't sleeve cards that feed no verified line. What survives is whatever makes the proven machines better — the Reservoir kill, the Approach plan B, the punisher clock. Six checker-passed stacks and a 10,000-game goldfish log are the filter; taste never got a vote.

The top of the list buys certainty where the ledgers already point. Mana Vault leads: the loop is verified mana-neutral and its mana-positive extension is refuted, so a rock that casts for {1} and taps for {C}{C}{C} is the one candidate that flips the sign — every recast another Reservoir trigger toward the lethal. Teferi, Time Raveler is the redundancy the shield lacks: Grand Abolisher has no backup, and a static that holds opponents to sorcery speed covers the whole go-turn — and survives the creature sweepers a two-mana body doesn't.

Two entries carry their own conditions, and the conditions are the point. Sphinx of the Final Word comes in at two or more heavy-permission seats — its static makes the second Approach cast counter-proof, though it is no Abolisher substitute, since a Reservoir activation is not a cast. No More Lies comes in at fast-combo pods, where the slowest symmetric engine is pure fuel for the storm seat. Read each entry's condition before you shop: on this list, when is as load-bearing as what.

This ten reinforces what is already proven: the Aetherflux Reservoir kill off the Hullbreaker loop (stacks 004/006) gets a mana-positive rock, a turn-shield, and a token payoff; the Approach plan B (stack 002) gets counter-proofing; the punisher core gets a table-wide refuel.
