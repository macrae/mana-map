FROM THE EDITOR

THE ENGINE ISN'T INVITED

The Dinosaur is on the cover and the deck is named after him. The card that actually runs the thing is a four-mana Human that none of the tribal furniture in this list can search, shield or reveal.

MANA MAP
From the Editor
Vol. 7 · GISHATH, SUN'S AVATAR

The deck is named after the Dinosaur on the cover. The engine is a Human with three toughness who is not invited to a single one of the parties this deck throws.

That sentence is why Gishath, Sun's Avatar got an issue. On top is the plan everyone can see: six or seven turns of quiet land-playing under an eight-mana Avatar who, when he connects, turns combat damage into a fistful of Dinosaurs pulled off the top of your own library and dropped straight onto the table. Underneath it runs a second machine that needs none of that — shoot your own board for one point, and four creatures turn a scratch into a card, a land, an exile and a fresh 5/5. The scratch wipes off at end of turn. You paid nothing.

You will have a good time here if you like the long sit, if you enjoy hurting your own creatures on purpose, and if you can be patient with a commander who is expensive and honest about it. You will not, if you need your commander to be your plan. Ledger has the cast turn, and it is no comfort.

Coach Brightside opens the log by naming the card to mulligan for, and it is not the one on the cover. The Counselor spends the rest of the page keeping him inside what has been proved — and the most interesting sentence in this file is that nobody has yet resolved a board of these Dinosaurs killing anybody. Read that as an invitation.

Your commander is the only card in this deck you are guaranteed to have. He waits in the command zone, and you may cast him from there for as long as the game lasts (CR 903.4). That is why no opening hand in this issue needs him, and why a 65-spell list can afford to point half its mana at one eight-drop.

Now the ladder. Every cast from the zone after the first costs {2} more (CR 903.8): eight, then ten, then twelve. Ledger measures mean available mana at 5.486 on turn six and 7.321 on turn ten. Read those two facts side by side — the average turn-ten board cannot pay the first cast, never mind the second.

So buy the shield, not the recast. Flawless Maneuver costs you nothing while you control your commander, and Heroic Intervention costs two. Either one is cheaper than the rider you pay for letting him die, and that is the trade this deck takes every single time.

Colour identity governs the rest of it. Every card in the 99 sits inside white, red and green (CR 903.4, 903.5c), which buys the best board-wide protection instants in the format and the whole enrage package. It also locks out counterspells permanently. When At the Table calls the combo seat a race, this clause is the reason.

The short clock is real here rather than incidental. Twenty-one combat damage from the same commander eliminates a player (CR 903.10a), and yours swings for seven with vigilance, trample and haste. Three connections end a seat — two if Overwhelming Stampede or a Kessig Wolf Run activation stretches the arithmetic. Count both clocks before you declare attackers.

And the political read, because the zone is also a stage. Nobody wraths a board of Signets, and nobody calls you the archenemy while the big man is still an idea. The table's patience is a resource, and you are spending it on purpose. Spend six turns of it, then cast him with a shield in hand and no apology.

THE PLAN

WHO NEEDS HIM?

Your commander never lands by turn ten in 5,533 of 10,000 games — so what is the deck doing in all of those, and why does it still win them? Two engines, and only one of them costs eight.

The table reads {5}{R}{G}{W} and files you under later. The goldfish says otherwise: Gishath, Sun's Avatar has a mean cast turn of 7.362, lands by turn six in 25.5% of games, and never lands by turn ten in 2,299 of 10,000. Every turn before him is THE EIGHT-MANA CLIMB, and it is not a waiting room.

He is the whole engine. Three cards make the command-zone cast cheaper — Otepec Huntmaster {1}, Urza's Incubator {2}, Knight of the Stampede {2} — and all three together leave exactly {R}{G}{W}. Then THE FIRST CONNECTION: vigilance, trample and haste are printed on him, so the kit's job is a clear lane (Rogue's Passage, Kessig Wolf Run) and a bigger hit (Enlarge is +7/+7, a fourteen-card reveal). Forge, 40 games against sythis, jarad and abaddon: 60 attacks, 42 reveals, 0.70 per attack. At that rate the goldfish fires the reveal 1.953 times a game for 4.096 free Dinosaurs.

Stack 001 is what one seven-damage hit buys: four Dinosaurs off the top enter as one event, three cards off Garruk's Uprising, one Pantlaza discover at X=6 — and X=6 only because you stack the Regisaur Alpha token trigger last. That is A CARD PER DINOSAUR, and it is the only verified arrow that starts from him.

The enrage web is gone. What is left is two converters, Trapjaw Tyrant and Wrathful Raptors, and one card that damages your own board without a mana cost per hit, Blasphemous Act. Read the pair for what it is: the Act through Temple Altisaur is a one-sided 13 (stack 004) that arrives in 8.1% of games unassisted, 29.9% with a tutor. An answer to a wide table, not a plan.

What the table misreads: you deploy in one wave, so one sweeper takes the board and the dorks under it. Kill by turn 8 is 0.617 against a goldfish with no blockers; with blockers, Forge gave 12 wins in 35 decided games. Hold Flawless Maneuver over the tap-out turn — with him on the battlefield it costs nothing.

What this does not model

  • Every arrow into the wincon stage is drawn dashed. Ten lines in the schematic, four rest on a passing stack, and not one of the five stacks resolves combat damage to a player.
  • The only measured attack on a life total in this file is stack 004: sixteen redirected damage takes one seat from 26 to 10, and that seat is alive at the end of it.
  • The commander's mean cast turn is 7.969, and 5,533 of 10,000 seeded games never cast him by turn ten at all — with no cost reduction modelled, so that is a floor on the problem rather than the expectation.
  • Unsettled Sunfrill Imitator attacks alongside Gishath and its attack trigger resolves, making it a copy of Gishath except for its name: does the copy have the combat-damage reveal trigger this combat, is it a 7/6 with vigilance, trample and haste, and does the legend rule ever apply given the names differ?
  • Unsettled Eerie Interlude cast in response to a sweeper with Pantlaza, Garruk's Uprising, Vaultborn Tyrant, Earthshaker Dreadmaw and four other Dinosaurs: all return at the next end step as one event — how many Uprising and Vaultborn draws, how many Dreadmaw draws, and exactly one Pantlaza discover at which X?
  • Unsettled What board actually kills a 40-life opponent, and from how far? No checker-passed stack resolves a lethal swing; the closest measurement is stack 004's 16 redirected damage taking one opponent from 26 to 10.
  • Simulation 10,000 runs of resource development, not of games — no opponent acts in any of them.

2 further questions about this engine are open and unsettled.

SIXTY-SIX HANDS

No artist owns this deck. Sixty-six painters share eighty-nine cards, and the largest single share anyone holds is three — so the honest story here is breadth, and the ledger likes breadth.

Call it a plurality, not a dynasty. Daarken holds three cards, which ties for the most anyone has in this deck, and three of eighty-nine is not a signature — it is a coincidence with good timing.

The timing is what makes it worth a page. Two of those three are Blasphemous Act and Temple Altisaur, which are the two halves of the one-sided sweep the Counselor resolved in stack 004. No foils, no borderless printings, no gallery to hang. What this deck has instead is sixty-six different people, which is its own kind of good.
