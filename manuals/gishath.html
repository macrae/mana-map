THE PLAN

ACT ONE IS A BLUFF

They see an eight-mana commander and they relax. Good — while they wait for act two, you're loading two engines that pay off whether or not the big turn ever comes.

Let me tell you what the table thinks you're playing, and then what you're actually playing. They see an eight-mana commander and they relax. Good. Let them.

This deck is a two-act play, and Gishath, Sun's Avatar is only the second act. Act one is six quiet turns of ramp with a smile on your face: 35 lands, five rocks, three dorks, five land-ramp spells, three cost reducers. Meanwhile the deck assembles two engines, each one turning a single small event into a pile of material. You are not behind during those turns -- you are loading.

Engine one is the headline. When Gishath connects, you reveal that many cards, and every Dinosaur among them hits the battlefield free. That's not hope -- that's stack 001, which resolves one 7-damage connection into five new bodies, three Garruk's Uprising draws, and a Pantlaza discover at X=6. The sequencing lesson rides along: take the discover off your biggest toughness BEFORE any token enters, because Pantlaza's once-a-turn clause means the trigger you waste is a trigger you never get back.

Engine two doesn't need combat at all: the enrage ping web. With Forerunner of the Empire on the table, any Dinosaur entering lets you ping every creature for 1. Stack 005 shows one voluntary ping buying an exiled blocker (Trapjaw Tyrant), a card (Ripjaw Raptor), a land (Ranging Raptors), a fresh 5/5 (Polyraptor), and a dead opposing token -- all for damage that wipes off at cleanup. One ping, five payoffs, and the Polyraptor copy re-arms the trigger.

Now the coach's honesty, because I only promise wins I can deliver. The naked numbers say Gishath's mean cast turn is 7.969, and he's down by turn six in just 8.3% of simulated games. But that simulation can't even see Selvala, Regal Behemoth, or your reducers -- treat it as the floor of the problem, not your ceiling. What it CAN see is your act-one machinery firing: ramp in hand by turn six in 86.9% of games, the enrage engine in 72.6%, a Dinosaur payoff in 62.3%, a reducer or cheat piece in 56.2%.

Your job in act one is to spend that abundance while respecting the one thing that beats you: the board wipe. This deck deploys in one wave -- the trigger IS the wave -- so you swing big only with a shield held. You'll have one by turn six about 48.5% of the time; play like it's coming. When somebody does wipe you, rebuild the web first: it restarts at two cheap cards, while the commander plan restarts at eight plus tax.

And you have your own wipe that isn't really a wipe. Stack 004 verifies Blasphemous Act under Temple Altisaur and Wrathful Raptors as a one-sided apocalypse that throws 16 damage at a player on the way through.

Last thing, and this is your pregame speech. The bracket report computes a floor of 4, driven entirely by a database tag calling Polyraptor plus Forerunner a two-card infinite. Stack 003 put that tag under oath and it came back bounded: a maximum of SEVEN tokens, reached only by declining one trigger, and the chain kills its own engine on the third sweep. So you present the deck at 4 and you argue it at 3 -- out loud, before the game, with the verified line in hand.

That conversation isn't a weakness. That conversation is you running the table before a single land hits it.

His own sweep hits him too — verified at 1 of his 3 toughness in stack 005, and dead to his own third sweep in stack 003. The only umbrellas that cover him are your board-wide instants: Flawless Maneuver, Heroic Intervention, Boros Charm, Akroma's Will, or an Eerie Interlude blink. So deploy him the turn he works, not a turn early. A sharp opponent kills the 1/3 before the 7/6.

When all three boxes check and the moment comes: cast, connect, bank the trigger, and welcome the coalition. You didn't spend six turns under the radar to flinch on turn eight. Archenemy with a stocked board and a free Maneuver is exactly the job this deck was built to hold.

Exactly ten cards make this list — four already in the box, six scouted from the wider pool, every one ranked with its evidence attached. The top picks buy no new tricks: they extend the verified enrage web and turn the reveal you already trust into damage.

Here's how the ten below got picked, because a ranked list is only as honest as its assembly line. The bench went under the lamp first: twenty-one fair, conditional bodies, and exactly four earn a slot by plugging into engines the stacks verify — the lead extends the stack 005 ping web directly. The other six are scouted from the pool to fill gaps the indexes flag. Each one beat the bench tail on a claim the tail couldn't match: a verified-stack extension, an index entry, or a role the budget says the deck is short.

Read the evidence tags like a balance sheet. A stack number next to a claim is settled money — the line already resolved under the checker. A goldfish or index figure is reproducible arithmetic: solid, but it measures the deck as built, not the deck plus the new card. And 'needs a stack scenario' is a promissory note — the biggest ceilings on this list carry that flag, and you don't book a ceiling until the scenario runs.

Every entry names its natural cut and its bracket delta, and one number frames all ten: none of them moves the computed floor off 4.
