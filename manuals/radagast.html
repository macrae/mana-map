FROM THE EDITOR

THE DECK THAT WAITS

Nine issues in, this is the first deck whose best turn is somebody else's.

MANA MAP
From the Editor
Vol. 9 · RADAGAST

The thing I want you to notice in this issue is a creature that never attacks.

It stands on the board where this deck actually kills somebody, it has defender printed on it, it deals nothing, and it is the reason the five creatures beside it add up to exactly enough. Coach Brightside opens the log with it and spends the page being argued with. He is right, he is narrower than he thinks, and watching him take the correction is the best thing in the department.

Which is the deck. Radagast of Rhosgobel makes the first creature spell you cast each turn cheap and instant, and "each turn" turns out to mean everybody's. You sit with your hands full while three other people take their turns, spending the turns that were never yours — then, once, you spend your own.

You will have a good time here if you would rather be the seat nobody wants to test than the seat with the fastest clock, and if you find the pause pleasurable: the player on your left looking at your untapped Forests and going somewhere else. You will not, if your idea of a green deck is tapping out and finding out. This one asks you to sit on your hands holding six mana, which is either the best evening in the game or none of it.

Ledger has the rates for how often the board gets there, and they are not comforting. The Counselor has the arithmetic, and the margin is zero. Read both before you shuffle.

Margot Stet

Radagast of Rhosgobel resolves on turn four and nothing happens. No card, no token, no trigger — a 2/5 Wizard standing in front of four Forests at a table whose scariest memory of you is a Llanowar Elves. Three opponents file him under ramp and go back to the game they were already playing. That is the most useful thing this commander does, and he does it before he has done anything.

The guarantee is what makes it plannable. He starts in the command zone (CR 903.6) and you may cast him from there (CR 903.8), so the engine is never a topdeck and never a mulligan decision — and the consequence is legible in the 99 rather than in the rules. This deck runs exactly one true second copy of its engine (Yeva, Nature's Herald) and spends nine slots on keeping the first one alive. A deck that had to draw its discount would be built the other way round. The simulation has him down on a mean cast turn of 4.015, and by turn six in 94.1% of ten thousand runs — and it never cast a spell on anybody else's turn, so that is the floor, not the rate.

Exhibit one: the text. The first creature spell you cast each turn costs {2} less and can be cast as though it had flash. The load-bearing words are 'each turn' — the printed template 'each of your turns' exists, and Radagast does not use it. A turn is a structural unit every player takes (CR 500.1), so the count zeroes at every untap, yours and theirs alike: four windows per round in a four-player pod. One candour the record keeps: no single rule states that an each-turn count resets. The holding is derived from the ability's text plus the definition of a turn, and the checker judged the derivation sound exactly as hedged (stack 001).

Now price the recast. The ladder beside this page prints the mana — four, six, eight, ten (CR 903.8) — and mana is the smaller half of that bill, because this deck's whole output is mana it did not spend. Six is a full turn's worth around turn seven (mean available mana: 5.496 on turn six, 6.036 on turn seven), and a turn you empty is a round in which three of your four windows never open. One re-buy costs four windows, not two mana. The third rung barely exists: eight is above this deck's mean available mana on every turn the model runs, turn ten included at 7.415. One land argues with that — Castle Garenbrig's six green is spendable only on creature spells and creatures' abilities, and a commander cast from the zone is a creature spell, tax and all, the tax being an additional cost inside the same total cost (CR 601.2f). Stack 005 has that mana paying a creature spell mid-cast; nobody has yet filed the two halves as one case.

Coach's ledger on the same question: insure, don't re-buy. Guardian Augmenter is the shape of it — flashed in while the removal spell is still on the stack, itself a creature spell Radagast discounts, and it leaves your commanders hexproof, so the insurance arrives through the window it exists to protect. Tyvar's Stand does that job for one mana at X=0. Sylvan Safekeeper charges a land instead, cheap on the turn you need it and real in a deck whose land drops are down to 56.5% by turn six. Yeva, Nature's Herald covers the other failure: while she is out, answering Radagast no longer switches the whole thing off.

Colour identity built this 99 (CR 903.4), and mono-green makes it one line — 82 green pips across 63 of the 64 spells, 36 sources, nothing to fix. What it locks out is the 'no' half of Magic: zero counterspells, and Veil of Summer is the closest thing to a refusal on offer. What Radagast hands back is stranger. He discounts creature spells only, so every answer in here wears a body — and then he gives those bodies instant speed. That is how a mono-green deck acquires a sweeper it can cast during someone else's attack: Whiptongue Hydra is six mana at sorcery speed anywhere else, and once a turn here it costs {3}{G}, casts as though it had flash, and the sky empties.

Is commander damage a plan (CR 903.10a)? A coincidence at best. A 2/5 needs eleven connections, and Guardian Augmenter only brings that to six — more turns than this table will hand you. Your clock is X=6 and trample; the commander damage worth counting is somebody else's.

The political read is the department's last word. The table sees a four-mana 2/5 Wizard and 36 lands, thirty of them basic Forests: fair stompy, zero combos, zero Game Changers, computed floor 1. Let them keep believing it. After he resolves, the true text of this deck is two untapped Forests and a pass.

THE PLAN

NEVER TAP OUT

Turn four, six Forests open, and the table has already filed you under ramp. Every untapped Forest is a loaded question — and the answer, four times a round, is a discounted body nobody voted for.

How many turns a round does this deck actually get? The table says one. Radagast makes the first creature spell you cast each turn cost {2} less and lets you cast it at instant speed, and "each turn" means every player's turn — four windows a round at a four-player table, verified in stack 001, where Wood Elves costs a single {G} on an opponent's end step. Stop waiting for your own turn.

Here is the trade the deck makes. Hold the mana you were not going to spend, and buy a body and a card on a turn that is not yours: Beast Whisperer, Toski and Glademuse all pay you for a cast you were making anyway, and Glademuse pays only when it is not your turn — which is exactly where you are casting. The goldfish average board at turn six is 3.8 bodies and it counts no window at all, so every window you take is a body nobody modelled. Take all four.

The same open mana is your interaction, which is why holding it costs you nothing. Ohran Frostfang flashed in after blockers are declared hands your attackers deathtouch in time for damage and draws off it (stack 002); Fecundity turns their wrath into four cards while indestructible Toski just stands there (stack 004). Spend each window on whatever hurts the table most that turn — a body, an answer, a blowout. The board that survives all of that is the win condition; everything after it is collection.

So treat Craterhoof Behemoth as a button, not a plan. Press it from your own precombat main — flashed on an end step, the pump wears off before you get to swing with it (stack 005). Five other bodies beside the Hoof clears exactly one seat's forty and four bodies leaves that seat alive on 4 (stacks 005 and 003), so choose the seat first, then subtract every creature they can put in front of you. What else that count quietly assumes is printed beside this page — read it before you press.

What this does not model

  • Stack 003 measures the four-body board: 36 unblocked into a 40-life seat. Opponent 1 survives at 4. That is the floor, and the floor loses.
  • Stack 005's kill is exactly 40 trampling power into ONE seat — per seat, never the pod. There is no margin in it.
  • Both figures are computed over boards where the target seat has no creatures and nobody blocks, nobody removes the finisher, and three opponents take no action for a full turn cycle.
  • Unsettled Whiptongue Hydra flashed in after blockers are declared: does its ETB sweep the fliers before combat damage, how many +1/+1 counters does it keep, and does it kill your own Hornet Nest tokens and Hornet Queen tokens in the process?
  • Unsettled Can Scute Swarm alone reach the fifth body BESIDE the finisher? With six or more lands its landfall creates a copy of itself per land entering; how many bodies does the board hold on the turn before Craterhoof, counting one land drop plus a fetch from Wood Elves or Sakura-Tribe Elder?
  • Unsettled Does Mirkwood Pathmaker function as this deck's greatest-power dial? With N lands, what is the X on Overwhelming Stampede and Pathbreaker Ibex, how many cards does Return of the Wildspeaker draw, and does Selvala's 'power greater than each other creature's power' gate then shut off for every subsequent body — including against opponents' creatures?
  • Simulation 10,000 runs of resource development, not of games — no opponent acts in any of them.

4 further questions about this engine are open and unsettled.
