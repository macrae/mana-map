The Game Plan

by Coach Sunny Brightside

THE PLAN

CAST EVERYTHING BUT HIM

What is the strongest card in a deck that begins every game with it face up and never pays a mana for it? Edgar Markov makes a Vampire for every Vampire you cast — from the command zone, from turn 1, for free — and version 2 changed what the banked bodies are for: the game that goes long is now, for the first time, your game.

Start with the number that defines this deck: zero. That is what the engine costs. Edgar Markov's eminence triggers from the command zone — every other Vampire spell you cast makes a 1/1 black Vampire token while he sits at home, untaxed and untouchable. The goldfish run (seed 42, 10,000 iterations, conservative — no interaction modeled) shows what that buys: a mean of 1.766 bodies by turn 3 and 4.111 by turn 6, on a curve that stacks 8 one-drops and 18 each at two and three. And in v2 the bodies themselves became ammunition twice over: Purphoros, God of the Forge turns every entry into 2 damage to each opponent — stack 008 verified that one two-mana Vampire cast through both doublers is five entry triggers, 10 damage to every opponent — and the aristocrats layer (Viscera Seer, Cruel Celebrant, Grave Pact) converts each spare token into a scry, a table-wide drain, and an edict on its way out. You are the beatdown by default, and a sweeper now costs the table almost as much as it costs you.

Combat reach is the each-opponent drain, verified at both ends of the turn. Stack 003: seven attacking Vampires under Sanctum Seeker and Bloodletter of Aclazotz drain each opponent 14 at declare-attackers, before blockers exist. Stack 007: the same seven tapped bodies drain each opponent another 14 at your end step through Throne of the God-Pharaoh — with the honest bound its own artifact insists on: that end-step trigger is not a table-kill (a 15-life opponent survives it at exactly 1), and the 28-per-opponent full-turn figure is arithmetic composition of two separately verified artifacts, not one verified line. A drain engine is drawn by turn 6 in 62.7% of games; a lord or Vampire payoff in 66.3%; a token amplifier in 42.2%, and stack 002's math still governs the doubler turns: one two-mana Vampire cast becomes five bodies through Anointed Procession and Mondrak.

The headline change of v2: the infinite graduated from insurance to co-equal plan. The deck now holds a 2x2 kill-web — two lose-halves (Vito, Thorn of the Dusk Rose; Sanguine Bond) times two gain-halves (Exquisite Blood; Bloodthirsty Conqueror) — and all four pairings are checker-verified deterministic table-kills (stacks 001, 004, 005, 006), each a zero-mana loop once both pieces are down. Natural assembly of any pair is 5.4% by turn 6 and 9.4% by turn 10 with tutors unmodeled, and Demonic Tutor, Vampiric Tutor, and Diabolic Intent each count as a copy of whichever half you're missing. Sequencing is now a skill of its own: stack 005's pairing — Sanguine Bond plus Exquisite Blood, both enchantments — is the one instant-speed creature removal cannot break, so when the table holds up mana, tutor toward that pairing first. Redundancy, not any single combo, is what the seven swaps bought: for the first time the game that goes long is often your game.

Edgar himself is still the pivot, not the opener. His goldfish median cast is turn 6 (mean 6.628, 51.2% by turn 6), and the correct pivot is casting him the turn you alpha-strike: his attack trigger puts a +1/+1 counter on every Vampire you control, converting an accumulated token board into lethal width. Hold protection for exactly that turn — Akroma's Will, Boros Charm, Flawless Maneuver; a protection piece is held by turn 6 in 57.5% of games — and remember the tension the strategic frame names: Deflecting Swat and Flawless Maneuver are free only while you control a commander, which is precisely what the eminence plan delays. Beat down, drain wide, burn every cast through Purphoros, and finish with whichever half of the web the table failed to answer.

Every cast is two bodies — the creature and the eminence token — and the payoffs find themselves: a lord or Vampire payoff is drawn by turn 6 in 66.3% of simulated games. Do not cast Edgar. The engine costs nothing while he sits at home.

Deploy under wrath-math — commit what wins, no further — knowing the sweep now costs the table almost as much as it costs you.

The attack turn: cast Edgar (median turn 6) into a protected seven-wide swing — 14 from every opponent at declare (stack 003), another 14 at your end step (stack 007, verified reach, not a kill), counters from Edgar's own trigger on top. Or the stack: tutor the missing half of the 2×2 loop matrix — and when the table holds open mana, fetch toward Sanguine Bond + Exquisite Blood, the pairing instant-speed creature removal cannot break.

THE DRILL

NEVER KEEP FOR EDGAR

81.7% of first sevens are keeps at 0.219 mulligans per game — and your commander is none of the reason. He's face up in the zone before your first decision, so the drill is pure engine-hand arithmetic: enablers over payoffs, untapped over tapped, and the seductive new trap — a fistful of loop halves — shipped without mercy.

Keepable first sevens (simulated)82%

KEEP — the gold standard

Swamp, Concealed Courtyard, Blood Crypt, Knight of the Ebon Legion, Viscera Seer, Legion Lieutenant, Night's Whisper

Three lands that can all enter untapped, two one-mana Vampires (each worth two bodies), a lord, and a refill. This is the enabler-heavy shape that wins the engine-hand test — the payoffs find themselves: a lord or payoff is drawn by turn 6 in 66.3% of games.

KEEP — the turbo two-lander

Ancient Tomb, Swamp, Sol Ring, Vampire of the Dire Moon, Cordial Vampire, Herald's Horn, Sanctum Seeker

Two lands reads risky until you price the mana: Tomb into Ring is four mana of ramp on turn 1, and Herald's Horn discounts every body behind it while digging at each upkeep. The deck-facts nuance that saves this keep — 16 of 34 lands can enter tapped, so a two-land hand needs its lands untapped. These are.

SHIP — the combo mirage

Godless Shrine, Savai Triome, Nomad Outpost, Sanguine Bond, Exquisite Blood, Vito, Thorn of the Dusk Rose, Olivia's Wrath

Three loop halves in one opener feels like destiny — and it is the classic all-payoff mulligan the frame warns about: no play before turn 3, the entire eminence dividend skipped, and your kill telegraphed from the first discard. Natural assembly runs 5.4% by turn 6 without any help, and the tutors are the real copies. Enablers are the thing an opener must actually hold.

SHIP — the flood the sim would keep

Swamp, Swamp, Caves of Koilos, Haunted Ridge, Sundown Pass, Teferi's Protection, Demonic Tutor

The goldfish's mechanical rule keeps 2–5 lands, so this five-lander passes the machine — and the coach overrules it. One spell to cast before turn 4, and protection plus a tutor are luxuries in an opener, not a plan. The badge difference printed honestly: ◆ says keepable, ★ says ship.

Run the engine-hand test the strategic frame prescribes: your enablers are cheap Vampire spells and your payoffs are lords, drain pieces, and loop halves — keep enabler-heavy hands, ship payoff-heavy ones. The classic all-payoff mulligan in v2 is the seductive one: two loop halves and no cheap Vampires does nothing for four turns and telegraphs your kill to boot. Concretely, keep two to four lands (the goldfish's own mulligan rule keeps 2-5, and 81.7% of first sevens pass; kept hands cluster at 2-4 lands) plus at least two Vampires you can cast in the first three turns. Those casts are worth double — a body and an eminence token each, and with the aristocrats layer maindecked, every token is now also stored value: a future scry, edict, and drain. The payoffs find themselves: a lord or Vampire payoff is drawn by turn 6 in 66.3% of games and a drain engine in 62.7%, so you never need to mulligan toward them. One noncreature card upgrades a marginal keep: Herald's Horn — the frame's best turn-3 play — discounts every body behind it and digs a Vampire off the top each upkeep, which is why ramp-or-fixing now reaches 50.2% by turn 6.

Never keep a hand for Edgar and never mourn his absence — he lives in the command zone, and the engine runs at full power while he sits there. Ship: zero-or-one-land hands, five-plus-land hands, and any hand whose spells all cost four or more, which does nothing for three turns in a deck built to bank a token per turn from turn one or two. One nuance from deck-facts: 16 of the 34 lands can enter tapped, so a two-land keep wants at least one land that comes in untapped for the one-drop start. Protection (held by turn 6 in 57.5% of runs) and a tutor are luxuries in an opener, not requirements — the free mulligan exists, and this deck at six cards with a working curve beats seven cards of top-end every time.

Pilot TipGrade one-drops on casts, not stats — Viscera Seer on turn 1 is a body, a banked eminence token, and a machine that will out-value most rares in this deck by turn 8.
He lives in the command zone. Mulligan like he doesn't exist.

The line

Coalition risk
High and accepted. Read
This board is the textbook pivot window — 'The window opens when your win attempt either can't be profitably answered — protection up, table tapped out, interaction counted and accounted for' — and you can count it: Player 3 is tapped out and dies, Player 4 holds two mana, and Player 2's wrath is covered by a zero-mana Flawless Maneuver that Edgar's own resolution turned on. That last point is the whole architecture of this deck: the free-protection suite is priced in Edgar, so the turn you cast him is the turn you are safest, not the turn you are most exposed. The honest costs: a counterspell on Edgar is the one clean beat (the drains still happened next turn without him — you attack with six anyway), and a -X/-X or exile sweeper goes under indestructible — if Player 2's deck is known for Toxic Deluge rather than destroy effects, discount Flawless accordingly and this branch loses some shine. And know what a wrath actually costs you: the four tokens are gone forever, but the recast tax math says Edgar dying once is survivable — eminence rebuilds tokens per cast from the zone regardless — and in the v2 list the retaliatory wrath is partially monetized before it even resolves, since Grave Pact and Cruel Celebrant sit in the 99 waiting to convert your next swept board into edicts and drains.

This branch is right in one specific world: concrete evidence Player 2's four lands are bluff (they discarded at cleanup, or their known list runs sorcery-speed answers only). Absent that read, it is the greedy line wearing the patient line's clothes.

Read
Understand exactly where this line loses: not to enchantment removal, but to a kill spell on Vito. Every iteration of the loop is a triggered ability that goes on the stack and passes priority — 'Once an ability has triggered, its controller puts it on the stack as an object that's not a card the next time a player would receive priority' — so Player 2 gets a window before the first Vito trigger resolves, and Vito dying mid-loop ends it (your gain continues off Exquisite Blood; the conversion to their loss is gone). Stack 001's table-kill is a fact about the loop, not about this table: its scenario stipulated no instant-speed interaction held, and Player 2's five lands are the negation of that premise. The v2 kill-web sharpens the point rather than softening it: stack 005 verified that Sanguine Bond + Exquisite Blood — both enchantments — kills identically (26 iterations) with no creature half for a kill spell to hit, which means presenting the one pairing that DIES to five open mana of creature interaction, when the deck contains a pairing that doesn't, is a sequencing error, not boldness. The pivot-window rule is written for exactly this moment: 'assume every held answer appears the moment you present lethal, and count the table's open mana before committing.' Five open mana from a proven interaction deck fails that count. This branch becomes correct when the count changes — Player 2 tapped out, or Deflecting Swat free because Edgar is on the battlefield — and naming those preconditions is the point of rejecting it today.

THE BENCH, AFTER THE RAID

Last issue's bench analysis didn't stay analysis: four case files ran, four passed, and seven cards walked into the 99 with verification stamps in hand. What's left is a true sideboard — two conditionals with named triggers, a recall shelf the swaps knowingly created, and one card that can never legally play.

The nine-swap analysis this section used to preview is now history — the deck you are holding is the result. The v1 bench analysis ranked nine forward-looking swaps under a recorded mandate (engine-extending picks only, verify before believing), and four of its claims were exactly the kind this manual refuses to print unverified: the two Sanguine Bond loops, the Throne-under-Bloodletter doubling, and the Purphoros per-entry count. So the verifications ran first. All four passed the checker at iteration 1 — stacks 005, 006, 007 (with its honest partially-refuted-as-a-table-kill bound), and 008 — and on 2026-07-28 the seven surviving swaps were applied: Viscera Seer, Sanguine Bond, Cruel Celebrant, Purphoros, Kalitas, Herald's Horn, and Throne of the God-Pharaoh in; Vampire Interloper, Stensia Masquerade, Gifted Aetherborn, Akroma's Memorial, Vampire Nighthawk, Ivora, and Blade of the Bloodchief to the bench. Every delta computed 4-to-4: the floor never moved, because the upgrade was never about the floor — it was redundancy on the kill (four verified pairings where v1 had two, all through-verified) and velocity to reach it, which per the bracket barometers is the real power move inside bracket 4.

What remains on the bench is now two tiers. The conditional tier is Elenda, the Dusk Rose and Indulgent Aristocrat, the two swaps from the old analysis deliberately not applied. Elenda (in for Mavren Fein) is the aristocrats package's wrath-math payoff — counters off every death anywhere, dying into a doubled army of lifelink tokens — and belongs in the list once your pods punish wide boards hard enough that threats must replace themselves. Indulgent Aristocrat (in for Vampire of the Dire Moon) is outlet redundancy once Viscera Seer proves out, at the named cost of the deck's last one-drop deathtouch defender — which is why it was the ninth swap and not the second, and why the thinned-defense tension the strategic frame flags argues for patience here. The seven fresh cuts sit beside them, and two are worth remembering at aggro-heavy tables: Gifted Aetherborn and Vampire Nighthawk are exactly the cheap deathtouch wall the swaps thinned, and re-boarding one is the natural meta call if your fair-combat seat keeps getting run over (a coaching judgment, not a computed one). Finally, four bench slots are spares, not options: a second Strefan, a second Vito, and two printings each of Vampire Nocturnus and Unholy Officiant — backup copies, sitting in the box.

Case A-001 Exquisite Blood + Vito: is the classic drain loop a table-kill?
Rules version 2026-06-19 · status: cleared in 1 review cycle(s) · tap to open Verified
  1. Seed: Vampire Nighthawk's lifelink combat damage to opponent 3 caused you to gain 2 life (you: 31 as given). Vito's ability ('Whenever you gain life, target opponent loses that much life') triggered on that gain and is on the stack. Its target — opponent 3, at 12 — was locked in when the trigger was put on the stack.
    One Vito trigger on the stack targeting opponent 3 for 2. Targets are per-instance choices made at put-on-stack time, not at resolution.
    CR 702.15b — “Damage dealt by a source with lifelink causes that source’s controller, or its owner if it has no controller, to gain that much life (in addition to any other results that damage causes).”
    CR 115.1d — “A triggered ability is targeted if it identifies something it will affect by using the phrase “target [something],” where the “something” is a phrase that describes an object and/or player. The target(s) are chosen as the ability is put on the stack; see rule 603.3d.”
  2. Vito's trigger resolves (no responses; opponents hold no instant-speed interaction). Opponent 3 loses 2 life: 12 -> 10. That life loss matches Exquisite Blood's trigger condition ('Whenever an opponent loses life, you gain that much life'), so Exquisite Blood triggers during resolution — but nothing happens yet.
    Opponent 3 at 10. Exquisite Blood has triggered and is waiting to be put on the stack.
    CR 117.2a — “Triggered abilities can trigger at any time, including while a spell is being cast, an ability is being activated, or a spell or ability is resolving. (See rule 603, “Handling Triggered Abilities.”) However, nothing actually happens at the time an ability triggers.”
  3. The next time a player would receive priority, state-based actions are checked (none apply — opponent 3 is at 10), then Exquisite Blood's trigger is put on the stack. It has no targets. It resolves: you gain 2 (31 -> 33). That life gain triggers Vito again.
    You gain 2 per iteration; each Exquisite Blood resolution re-triggers Vito. The engine is now self-sustaining.
    CR 603.3 — “Once an ability has triggered, its controller puts it on the stack as an object that’s not a card the next time a player would receive priority.”
    CR 117.5 — “Each time a player would get priority, the game first performs all applicable state-based actions as a single event (see rule 704, “State-Based Actions”), then repeats this process until no state-based actions are performed. Then triggered abilities are put on the stack”
  4. The new Vito trigger is put on the stack, and a NEW target is chosen for this new instance. You may keep targeting opponent 3 or retarget any other opponent — each iteration is an independent targeting decision, since target choice is part of putting each triggered ability on the stack.
    Retargeting between iterations is legal and free. You keep opponent 3 as the target while they live.
    CR 603.3d — “The remainder of the process for putting a triggered ability on the stack is identical to the process for casting a spell listed in rules 601.2c–d.”
    CR 115.1d — “The target(s) are chosen as the ability is put on the stack; see rule 603.3d.”
  5. Loop legality: each iteration (Vito resolves -> opponent loses 2 -> Exquisite Blood triggers -> resolves -> you gain 2 -> Vito triggers -> new target) is fully determined once you name the target, so the whole sequence may be proposed as a shortcut for a stated number of iterations rather than physically performed.
    The loop is deterministic and repeatable indefinitely while both permanents remain and a legal target exists. It transfers exactly 2 life per iteration from the targeted opponent to you.
    CR 732.1b — “Occasionally the game gets into a state in which a set of actions could be repeated indefinitely (thus creating a “loop”). In that case, the shortcut rules can be used to determine how many times those actions are repeated without having to actually perform them, and how the loop is broken.”
    CR 732.2a — “At any point in the game, the player with priority may suggest a shortcut by describing a sequence of game choices, for all players, that may be legally taken based on the current game state and the predictable results of the sequence of choices.”
  6. Killing opponent 3: after 6 total Vito resolutions targeting them (6 x 2 = 12), opponent 3 hits 0 life. Before any player next receives priority, state-based actions are performed: opponent 3 loses the game and leaves it.
    Opponent 3 is out. Their owned objects leave the game with them (none here).
    CR 704.5a — “If a player has 0 or less life, that player loses the game.”
    CR 104.5 — “If a player loses the game, that player leaves the game.”
    CR 800.4a — “When a player leaves the game, all objects (see rule 109) owned by that player leave the game”
  7. Does Exquisite Blood trigger off the loss that killed them? Yes. The life-loss event occurred while opponent 3 was still in the game, so Exquisite Blood triggered at that moment (though nothing happened yet); the ability is then put on the stack after state-based actions remove opponent 3, and it resolves normally — it targets nothing and belongs to YOUR enchantment. You gain 2 and Vito triggers again.
    The chain is not broken by a targeted opponent's death: the final loss still feeds a life-gain iteration, and the next Vito trigger simply chooses a new target among the survivors (603.3d would remove a Vito trigger only if NO legal target existed).
    CR 117.2a — “However, nothing actually happens at the time an ability triggers. Each time a player would receive priority, each ability that has triggered but hasn’t yet been put on the stack is put on the stack.”
    CR 117.5 — “the game first performs all applicable state-based actions as a single event (see rule 704, “State-Based Actions”), then repeats this process until no state-based actions are performed. Then triggered abilities are put on the stack”
    CR 603.3d — “If a choice is required when the triggered ability goes on the stack but no legal choices can be made for it, or if a rule or a continuous effect otherwise makes the ability illegal, the ability is simply removed from the stack.”
  8. Edge case for completeness: if a Vito trigger were somehow already on the stack targeting a player who then left the game, that instance would not resolve (illegal target). In this loop it never happens — exactly one trigger resolves at a time and each new one is targeted fresh.
    No iteration is wasted; the loop loses no life to fizzled triggers under correct sequencing.
    CR 608.2b — “If the spell or ability specifies targets, it checks whether the targets are still legal. A target that’s no longer in the zone it was in when it was targeted is illegal.”
  9. Continue the shortcut against the survivors: 13 iterations targeting opponent 2 (25 -> -1; the 13th iteration takes them from 1 to -1) and 19 iterations targeting opponent 1 (38 -> 0). Each dies to the same state-based action and leaves the game; opponent 1's 'irrelevant permanents' leave with them. The loop does NOT depend on Vampire Nighthawk: it only seeded the first gain, and even the seed trigger exists on the stack independently of Nighthawk once it triggered — the Vito/Exquisite Blood engine runs on the two named permanents alone.
    All three opponents reach 0 or less. The loop survives the seed creature's death (or its absence entirely — any future life gain restarts it).
    CR 704.5a — “If a player has 0 or less life, that player loses the game.”
    CR 800.4a — “When a player leaves the game, all objects (see rule 109) owned by that player leave the game”
    CR 113.7a — “Once activated or triggered, an ability exists on the stack independently of its source. Destruction or removal of the source after that time won’t affect the ability.”
  10. The 38th and final Vito resolution puts opponent 1 at 0. State-based actions: opponent 1 loses and leaves. You are the only player remaining, so you win the game immediately — the last Exquisite Blood trigger (off opponent 1's killing loss) never resolves because the game has already ended.
    GAME OVER: you win. Final Exquisite Blood resolutions: 37 of 38 (the last is mooted by the win). Your life at game end: 31 + 37 x 2 = 105.
    CR 704.5a — “If a player has 0 or less life, that player loses the game.”
    CR 104.2a — “A player still in the game wins the game if that player’s opponents have all left the game. This happens immediately and overrides all effects that would preclude that player from winning the game.”

Case A-002 Edgar's eminence through two doublers: how many bodies per cast?
Rules version 2026-06-19 · status: cleared in 1 review cycle(s) · tap to open Verified
  1. All players pass priority; the stack resolves top-down. Edgar Markov's eminence trigger (top) resolves before the Gifted Aetherborn spell (bottom).
    The eminence triggered ability begins resolving first; Gifted Aetherborn waits on the stack.
    CR 405.5 — “When all players pass in succession, the top (last-added) spell or ability on the stack resolves.”
  2. The eminence trigger resolves. Its intervening 'if' clause — 'if Edgar is in the command zone or on the battlefield' — is checked again on resolution; Edgar is still in the command zone, and the ability functions there because it states the zones it functions in.
    The trigger resolves from the command zone and instructs you to create a 1/1 black Vampire creature token: a single token-creation event for one token.
    CR 113.6b — “An ability that states which zones it functions in functions only from those zones.”
    CR 603.4 — “If the ability triggers, it checks the stated condition again as it resolves. If the condition isn’t true at that time, the ability is removed from the stack and does nothing.”
  3. Two replacement effects — Anointed Procession ('If an effect would create one or more tokens under your control, it creates twice that many of those tokens instead') and Mondrak, Glory Dominus ('If one or more tokens would be created under your control, twice that many of those tokens are created instead') — both want to modify this one token-creation event. You, the affected player, choose which applies first.
    You pick an order (it is symmetrical here); the chosen doubler modifies the event, then the other applies to the modified event.
    CR 616.1 — “If two or more replacement and/or prevention effects are attempting to modify the way an event affects an object or player, the affected object’s controller (or its owner if it has no controller) or the affected player chooses one to apply”
  4. Each doubler gets exactly one application to the event, so the math is 1 token → 2 (first doubler) → 4 (second doubler), identical in either order.
    The modified event creates four 1/1 black Vampire creature tokens; neither doubler applies a second time.
    CR 614.5 — “A replacement effect doesn’t invoke itself repeatedly; it gets only one opportunity to affect an event or any modified events that may replace that event.”
  5. The four tokens are put onto the battlefield. They are created, not cast, so no game event matches eminence's 'you cast another Vampire spell' trigger condition — eminence does not fire again for the tokens.
    Four Vampire tokens enter the battlefield under your control; no new eminence triggers.
    CR 701.7a — “To create one or more tokens with certain characteristics, put the specified number of tokens with the specified characteristics onto the battlefield.”
    CR 603.2 — “Whenever a game event or game state matches a triggered ability’s trigger event, that ability automatically triggers.”
  6. Vampire Socialite's static ability — 'As long as an opponent lost life this turn, each other Vampire you control enters with an additional +1/+1 counter on it' — is an 'enters with' replacement effect. An opponent lost 3 life earlier this turn, and each token is another Vampire you control, so each token enters with a +1/+1 counter.
    Each 1/1 token enters with one +1/+1 counter and is a 2/2.
    CR 614.1d — “Continuous effects that read “[This permanent] enters . . .” or “[Objects] enter [the battlefield] . . .” are replacement effects.”
    CR 122.1a — “A +X/+Y counter on a creature or on a creature card in a zone other than the battlefield, where X and Y are numbers, adds X to that object’s power and Y to that object’s toughness.”
  7. Before any player gets priority, state-based actions are checked: no creature has 0 or less toughness or lethal damage, so nothing happens and nothing dies. Cordial Vampire ('Whenever this creature or another creature dies, put a +1/+1 counter on each Vampire you control') is scenery here — no death event occurred, so it never triggers. All players pass again.
    No state-based actions, no Cordial Vampire triggers; the Gifted Aetherborn spell (now top of stack) resolves next.
    CR 704.3 — “Whenever a player would get priority (see rule 117, “Timing and Priority”), the game checks for any of the listed conditions for state-based actions, then performs all applicable state-based actions simultaneously as a single event.”
    CR 603.2g — “An ability triggers only if its trigger event actually occurs.”
  8. Gifted Aetherborn (a 2/3 Aetherborn Vampire with deathtouch and lifelink, no targets) resolves and enters the battlefield under your control. It is another Vampire you control entering while an opponent lost life this turn, so Vampire Socialite's replacement effect applies to it as well. It is not a token, so Anointed Procession and Mondrak are irrelevant to it.
    Gifted Aetherborn enters with an additional +1/+1 counter: a 3/4 deathtouch lifelink Vampire.
    CR 608.3a — “If the object that’s resolving has no targets, it becomes a permanent and enters the battlefield under the control of the spell’s controller.”
    CR 614.1d — “Continuous effects that read “[This permanent] enters . . .” or “[Objects] enter [the battlefield] . . .” are replacement effects.”
    CR 122.1a — “A +X/+Y counter on a creature or on a creature card in a zone other than the battlefield, where X and Y are numbers, adds X to that object’s power and Y to that object’s toughness.”
  9. Final state-based-action check before priority: all creatures have positive toughness and no damage marked; the stack is empty.
    Nothing dies; no further triggers; the game proceeds with five new Vampire bodies on your battlefield.
    CR 704.3 — “Whenever a player would get priority (see rule 117, “Timing and Priority”), the game checks for any of the listed conditions for state-based actions, then performs all applicable state-based actions simultaneously as a single event.”

Case A-003 Bloodletter + Sanctum Seeker: what does an attack actually drain?
Rules version 2026-06-19 · status: cleared in 2 review cycle(s) · tap to open Verified
  1. Declare attackers: all seven Vampires (Bloodletter of Aclazotz, Sanctum Seeker, four 1/1 Vampire tokens, Edgar Markov) are declared as attackers. Sanctum Seeker's ability ('Whenever a Vampire you control attacks, each opponent loses 1 life and you gain 1 life') triggers once for each attacking Vampire — seven separate trigger events, so SEVEN Sanctum Seeker triggers. Edgar Markov's ability ('Whenever Edgar attacks, put a +1/+1 counter on each Vampire you control') triggers once.
    Eight triggered abilities are waiting to go on the stack: 7 Sanctum Seeker drains + 1 Edgar counter trigger.
    CR 508.3a — “An ability that reads “Whenever [a creature] attacks, . . .” triggers if that creature is declared as an attacker.”
    CR 603.2c — “An ability triggers only once each time its trigger event occurs. However, it can trigger repeatedly if one event contains multiple occurrences.”
  2. All eight triggers are put on the stack. You control all of them, so you choose their relative order. Chosen order here: Edgar's counter trigger is placed last (on top), so it resolves first; the seven Sanctum Seeker triggers resolve after it, one at a time.
    Stack (top to bottom): Edgar counter trigger, then Sanctum Seeker triggers #1–#7.
    CR 603.3b — “each player, in APNAP order, puts each triggered ability they control with a trigger condition that isn’t another ability triggering on the stack in any order they choose”
  3. Edgar Markov's trigger resolves: put a +1/+1 counter on each Vampire you control. All seven attackers get a counter (tokens become 2/2, Sanctum Seeker 4/5, Bloodletter 3/5, Edgar 5/5).
    Power and toughness change ONLY. Sanctum Seeker's drain is a fixed 'each opponent loses 1 life' — it does not reference power — so Edgar's counters do NOT change the trigger drain at all (they only matter for later combat damage). Active player would then receive priority, so the game checks state-based actions; no life totals changed, nothing happens.
    CR 117.3b — “The active player receives priority after a spell or ability (other than a mana ability) resolves.”
  4. Sanctum Seeker trigger #1 resolves: each opponent would lose 1 life and you would gain 1 life. Bloodletter of Aclazotz ('If an opponent would lose life during your turn, they lose twice that much life instead') is a replacement effect — it is your turn, so each opponent's 1 life loss is replaced with 2. It applies once to each opponent's loss event and does not re-apply to its own result. Your life GAIN is untouched: Bloodletter's text only replaces an OPPONENT'S life LOSS, and your gain is neither an opponent's event nor a loss.
    Opponent 1: 21 → 19. Opponent 2: 14 → 12. You: 26 → 27 (gain 1, not doubled). Life totals adjust accordingly.
    CR 614.1a — “Effects that use the word “instead” are replacement effects.”
    CR 614.5 — “A replacement effect doesn’t invoke itself repeatedly; it gets only one opportunity to affect an event or any modified events that may replace that event.”
    CR 119.3 — “If an effect causes a player to gain life or lose life, that player’s life total is adjusted accordingly.”
  5. Between each trigger resolution the active player would receive priority, so state-based actions are checked each time. Sanctum Seeker triggers #2 through #6 resolve identically: each opponent loses 2 (1 doubled by Bloodletter — the replacement applies anew to each separate life-loss event, since each trigger's resolution is its own event), you gain 1 each time.
    Running totals after trigger #6: Opponent 1: 21 → 9 (six drains × 2). Opponent 2: 14 → 2. You: 26 → 32. At every SBA check through trigger #6, opponent 2 is at 12/10/8/6/4/2 — above 0 — so no one has lost the game yet and all triggers keep resolving.
    CR 704.3 — “Whenever a player would get priority (see rule 117, “Timing and Priority”), the game checks for any of the listed conditions for state-based actions, then performs all applicable state-based actions simultaneously as a single event.”
    CR 117.2d — “State-based actions happen automatically when certain conditions are met. See rule 704. They’re dealt with before a player would receive priority.”
  6. Sanctum Seeker trigger #7 (the last drain) resolves: each opponent loses 1, doubled to 2 by Bloodletter; you gain 1.
    Opponent 1: 9 → 7. Opponent 2: 2 → 0. You: 32 → 33. Total drain across all seven triggers: 14 to each opponent, 7 gained by you.
    CR 614.1a — “replacement effects use the word “instead” to indicate what events will be replaced with other events”
    CR 119.3 — “If an effect causes a player to gain life or lose life, that player’s life total is adjusted accordingly.”
  7. State-based action check after trigger #7 resolves (before any player receives priority): opponent 2 has 0 life and loses the game, then leaves the game.
    Opponent 2 is eliminated on exactly the SEVENTH Sanctum Seeker trigger — the last one (14 life ÷ 2 per trigger = 7 triggers). The kill is not 'partway': the drain uses opponent 2's last point of life on the final trigger.
    CR 704.5a — “If a player has 0 or less life, that player loses the game.”
    CR 104.3b — “If a player’s life total is 0 or less, that player loses the game the next time a player would receive priority.”
    CR 104.5 — “If a player loses the game, that player leaves the game.”
  8. Would remaining triggers survive an opponent's death? Here none remain (Edgar's trigger already resolved first and opponent 2 died on the final drain). But in general: when a player leaves the game, only THAT player's objects leave — you control the Sanctum Seeker triggers, so any still on the stack would remain and resolve, and their 'each opponent' would simply drain only the opponents still in the game.
    Opponent 2's departure removes their own objects only; your triggers are unaffected. Combat continues against opponent 1 only.
    CR 800.4a — “When a player leaves the game, all objects (see rule 109) owned by that player leave the game and any effects which give that player control of any objects or players end.”
