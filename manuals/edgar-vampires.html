THE PLAN

CAST EVERYTHING BUT HIM

What's the best card in a deck that starts every game with it face up and never pays a mana for it? Edgar Markov makes a Vampire for every Vampire you cast — from the command zone, from turn 1, for free — and thanks to four verified loops, the game that goes long is yours too.

Start with the number that defines this deck: zero. That is what the engine costs. Edgar Markov's eminence triggers from the command zone — every other Vampire spell you cast makes a 1/1 black Vampire token while he sits at home, untaxed and untouchable. The goldfish run (seed 42, 10,000 iterations, conservative — no interaction modeled) prices what that buys: a mean of 1.766 bodies by turn 3 and 4.111 by turn 6, on a curve stacking 8 one-drops and 18 each at two and three.

Those bodies are ammunition twice over. Purphoros, God of the Forge turns every entry into 2 damage to each opponent — stack 008 verified that one two-mana Vampire cast through both doublers is five entry triggers, 10 damage to every opponent. The aristocrats layer (Viscera Seer, Cruel Celebrant, Grave Pact) converts each spare token into a scry, a table-wide drain, and an edict on its way out. You are the beatdown by default, and a sweeper costs the table almost as much as it costs you.

Combat reach is the each-opponent drain, verified at both ends of the turn. Stack 003: seven attacking Vampires under Sanctum Seeker and Bloodletter of Aclazotz drain each opponent 14 at declare-attackers, before blockers exist. Stack 007: the same seven tapped bodies drain each opponent another 14 at your end step through Throne of the God-Pharaoh — with the honest bound its own artifact insists on: the end-step trigger is not a table-kill (a 15-life opponent survives it at exactly 1), and the 28-per-opponent full-turn figure is arithmetic composition of two separately verified artifacts, not one verified line. The engines show up on schedule: a drain engine is drawn by turn 6 in 62.7% of games, a lord or Vampire payoff in 66.3%, a token amplifier in 42.2% — and stack 002's math governs the doubler turns, where one two-mana Vampire cast becomes five bodies through Anointed Procession and Mondrak, Glory Dominus.

The long game is yours too. The deck holds a 2x2 kill-web — two lose-halves (Vito, Thorn of the Dusk Rose; Sanguine Bond) times two gain-halves (Exquisite Blood; Bloodthirsty Conqueror) — and all four pairings are checker-verified deterministic table-kills (stacks 001, 004, 005, 006), each a zero-mana loop once both pieces are down. Natural assembly of any pair is 5.4% by turn 6 and 9.4% by turn 10 with tutors unmodeled, and Demonic Tutor, Vampiric Tutor, and Diabolic Intent each count as a copy of whichever half you're missing. Here is the trap and the better line: when the table holds up mana, tutor toward stack 005's pairing — Sanguine Bond plus Exquisite Blood, both enchantments — because instant-speed creature removal cannot break it. Redundancy, not any single pairing, is your real edge: the game that goes long is often your game.

Edgar himself is the pivot, not the opener. His goldfish median cast is turn 6 (mean 6.628, 51.2% by turn 6), and the right pivot is casting him the turn you alpha-strike: his attack trigger puts a +1/+1 counter on every Vampire you control, converting an accumulated token board into lethal width. Hold protection for exactly that turn — Akroma's Will, Boros Charm, Flawless Maneuver; a protection piece is held by turn 6 in 57.5% of games — and respect the tension the strategic frame names: Deflecting Swat and Flawless Maneuver are free only while you control a commander, which is precisely what the eminence plan delays. Beat down, drain wide, burn every cast through Purphoros, and finish with whichever half of the web the table failed to answer.

THE DRILL

NEVER KEEP FOR EDGAR

He's face up in the command zone before your first decision, so the drill is pure engine-hand arithmetic: enablers over payoffs, untapped over tapped. The Coach grades four real hands; Ledger prints the rate — 81.7% of first sevens keep, at 0.219 mulligans a game.

Keepable first sevens (simulated)82%

KEEP — the gold standard

Swamp, Concealed Courtyard, Blood Crypt, Knight of the Ebon Legion, Viscera Seer, Legion Lieutenant, Night's Whisper

Three lands that can all enter untapped, two one-mana Vampires (each worth two bodies), a lord, and a refill. This is the enabler-heavy shape that wins the engine-hand test — the payoffs find themselves: a lord or payoff is drawn by turn 6 in 66.3% of games.

KEEP — the turbo two-lander

Ancient Tomb, Swamp, Sol Ring, Vampire of the Dire Moon, Cordial Vampire, Herald's Horn, Sanctum Seeker

Two lands reads risky until you price the mana: Tomb into Ring is four mana on turn 1, and Herald's Horn discounts every body behind it while digging at each upkeep. The nuance that saves this keep — 16 of the 37 lands can enter tapped, so a two-land hand needs its lands untapped. These are.

SHIP — the combo mirage

Godless Shrine, Savai Triome, Nomad Outpost, Sanguine Bond, Exquisite Blood, Vito, Thorn of the Dusk Rose, Olivia's Wrath

Three loop halves in one opener feels like destiny — and it is the classic all-payoff mulligan: no play before turn 3, the entire eminence dividend skipped, and your kill telegraphed from the first discard. The tutors are the real copies. Enablers are what an opener must actually hold.

SHIP — the flood the sim would keep

Swamp, Swamp, Caves of Koilos, Haunted Ridge, Sundown Pass, Teferi's Protection, Demonic Tutor

The goldfish's mechanical rule keeps 2-5 lands, so this five-lander passes the machine — and the Coach overrules it. One spell to cast before turn 4, and protection plus a tutor are luxuries in an opener, not a plan. The badge difference, printed honestly: the diamond says keepable, the star says ship.

Run the engine-hand test the strategic frame prescribes: your enablers are cheap Vampire spells and your payoffs are lords, drain pieces, and loop halves — keep enabler-heavy hands, ship payoff-heavy ones. The seductive trap is the all-payoff keep: two loop halves and no cheap Vampires does nothing for four turns and telegraphs your kill to boot. Concretely, keep two to four lands (the goldfish's own mulligan rule keeps 2-5, and 81.7% of first sevens pass; kept hands cluster at 2-4 lands) plus at least two Vampires you can cast in the first three turns. Those casts are worth double — a body and an eminence token each — and every token is stored value: a future scry, edict, and drain.

The payoffs find themselves. A lord or Vampire payoff is drawn by turn 6 in 66.3% of games and a drain engine in 62.7%, so you never need to mulligan toward them. One noncreature card upgrades a marginal keep: Herald's Horn — the frame's best turn-3 play — discounts every body behind it and digs a Vampire off the top each upkeep, a big reason ramp-or-fixing reaches 50.2% by turn 6.

Never keep a hand for Edgar and never mourn his absence — he lives in the command zone, and the engine runs at full power while he sits there. Ship: zero-or-one-land hands, five-plus-land hands, and any hand whose spells all cost four or more, which does nothing for three turns in a deck built to bank a token per turn from turn one or two. One nuance from deck-facts: 16 of the 37 lands can enter tapped, so a two-land keep wants at least one land that comes in untapped for the one-drop start. Protection (held by turn 6 in 57.5% of runs) and a tutor are luxuries in an opener, not requirements — the free mulligan exists, and this deck at six cards with a working curve beats seven cards of top-end every time.

Pilot TipGrade one-drops on casts, not stats — Viscera Seer on turn 1 is a body, a banked eminence token, and a machine that will out-value most rares in this deck by turn 8.
He lives in the command zone. Mulligan like he doesn't exist.

The line

High and accepted. You kill a player this turn, and the remaining game is 1-versus-2 with you as archenemy. You also removed the aggro deck that was racing you, and your engine survives the counterattack: eminence keeps producing whether Edgar lives or dies, and if he dies he returns to the zone with an 8-mana recast attached.
Read

This board is the textbook pivot window — 'The window opens when your win attempt either can't be profitably answered — protection up, table tapped out, interaction counted and accounted for' — and you can count it. Player 3 is tapped out and dies. Player 4 holds two mana. Player 2's wrath is covered by a zero-mana Flawless Maneuver that Edgar's own resolution turned on.

That last point is the whole architecture of this deck: the free-protection suite is priced in Edgar. The turn you cast him is the turn you are safest, not the turn you are most exposed.

The honest costs. A counterspell on Edgar is the one clean beat — the drains still happen without him, and you attack with six next turn anyway. A -X/-X or exile sweeper goes under indestructible, so if Player 2 is known for Toxic Deluge rather than destroy effects, discount Flawless accordingly and this branch loses some shine.

And price the wrath correctly. The four tokens are gone forever, but Edgar dying once is survivable — eminence rebuilds a token per cast from the zone regardless — and Grave Pact and Cruel Celebrant sit in the 99 waiting to convert your next swept board into edicts and drains. The retaliatory wrath is partially monetized before it even resolves.

Keep Edgar zoned; cast Bloodline Keeper and Vampire Socialite

The line
Resolve the eminence token, then spend all six mana on two more Vampire spells: Bloodline Keeper ({2}{B}{B}) and Vampire Socialite ({B}{R}). Two casts bank two more eminence tokens. Attack with the six eligible bodies for six Sanctum drains — Player 3 to 6, not dead. Pass with a nine-plus-permanent board and Keeper ready to pump out 2/2 flyers.
Signals sent
This is the 'engine, not alpha' read — but the table sees a board that is now visibly one Edgar away from killing someone. To a competent Player 2 this does not read quieter than the alpha line. It reads like next turn's version of it, and it invites the wrath NOW, while your commander is uncast and your protection unpaid.
Coalition risk
Medium this turn, maximal next. Nobody dies, so no one is forced to act — but a control player counting your board will not give you the untap. You also leave Player 3 alive at 6 to keep pressuring someone.
Read

The trap in this line is the free-protection tension the strategic frame names: Flawless Maneuver costs a real {2}{W} while Edgar sits in the zone, and this line spends every mana. You are ten permanents deep into a telegraphed sweeper with no cover.

'Commit exactly as much as wins before it arrives, no further' is the operative rule, and this commitment does not win. Six drains leave everyone alive, so you take maximum wrath exposure for incremental value.

Eminence does soften the blow — the engine functions from the command zone by its own terms, so the rebuild is real. And yes, Grave Pact, Cruel Celebrant, and Viscera Seer live in the 99 to convert swept tokens into edicts, drains, and selection. But none of those pieces is on THIS board. The monetization is a deck fact, not a turn fact — you are trading a countable kill for a bigger board you may not keep.

This branch is right in one specific world: concrete evidence Player 2's four lands are bluff (they discarded at cleanup, or their known list runs sorcery-speed answers only). Absent that read, it is the greedy line wearing the patient line's clothes.

Commit small: Socialite only, hold real-mana protection

The line
Resolve the eminence token, cast only Vampire Socialite ({B}{R}), and attack with the six eligible bodies — six drains, Player 3 to 6. Hold four mana: enough to hard-cast Flawless Maneuver ({2}{W}) through the wrath, with a mana spare. Edgar waits for turn 7.
Signals sent
Passing with open mana from the deck that has cast a spell every turn reads as protection held — which is exactly true, and it may genuinely deter the wrath a turn. But the table also sees you NOT casting Edgar with six up. An observant player reads that as fear of the sweep you haven't drawn through.
Coalition risk
Lowest of the three now, but every passive turn compounds toward Player 4 — the Simic engine drawing extra cards each turn is the seat that wins long games, and nobody at this table is pressuring them except you.
Read

This is defensible wrath-math — wave one stays committed, the rebuild package stays in hand, protection is up. And patience is not a losing posture in this deck: with four verified pairings and three tutors in the 99, your inevitability against draw-go control is real.

But audit what the caution buys on THIS board. You hold Flawless for a board of tokens and lords you could rebuild anyway — eminence re-banks a token per cast, and Bloodline Keeper is still in hand as the post-wrath threat. The alpha line holds the SAME protection for free and kills a player with it.

And the seat that profits from your quiet turn is not the control deck. It is Player 4's Simic draw engine, which compounds regardless of whose inevitability is better on paper.

Take this line when the pivot genuinely isn't there. If Player 3 were at 20 instead of 12, no one dies through the alpha, and holding protection over a non-lethal swing becomes correct — the kill-web means the long game is yours to play. Here, with a countable elimination on the table, this is a turn of rent paid to fear.

Our call: Cast Edgar now and alpha-strike with free Flawless Maneuver held

The three tests of the pivot window all pass. The win attempt is profitable even if answered — seven drains at declare attackers put 7 on every opponent and kill Player 3 before any wrath can intervene. The table's interaction is counted: Player 3 tapped out, Player 4 at two mana, Player 2's destroy-wrath covered by a {0} Flawless Maneuver that only exists because Edgar resolved. And failure leaves a rebuilt position: eminence functions from the command zone, every future Vampire cast re-banks a token, a died Edgar costs 8 next time, and the aristocrats layer means even a swept board pays you on the way out.

The goldfish makes the same argument from the other side. Edgar's median cast IS turn 6 (51.2% by t6, mean 6.628), and you hold protection in this window in 57.5% of games. The branches that keep him zoned either maximize wrath exposure with zero cover or pay a tempo tax to the one seat — the Simic engine — that profits from every quiet turn.

The kill-web buys you genuine lategame inevitability, but deferred inevitability never outbids a countable elimination with free protection held. Your threat level is structural; you cannot deflect your way out of it at six mana with a seven-attacker board. Take the label on the turn it kills a player.

The line

Coalition risk
Total. A presented infinite unites all three opponents instantly, and unlike the combat plan there is no partial credit: if the loop is broken you have spent your best tutor, seven mana, and every scrap of goodwill for an enchantment that reads 'kill my Vito next.'
Read

Know exactly where this line loses: not to enchantment removal, but to a kill spell on Vito. Every iteration of the loop is a triggered ability that goes on the stack and passes priority — 'Once an ability has triggered, its controller puts it on the stack as an object that's not a card the next time a player would receive priority' — so Player 2 gets a window before the first Vito trigger resolves, and Vito dying mid-loop ends it. Your gain continues off Exquisite Blood; the conversion to their loss is gone.

Stack 001's table-kill is a fact about the loop, not about this table. Its scenario stipulated no instant-speed interaction held, and Player 2's five lands are the negation of that premise.

The kill-web sharpens the point rather than softening it. Stack 005 verified that Sanguine Bond + Exquisite Blood — both enchantments — kills identically (26 iterations) with no creature half for a kill spell to hit. Presenting the one pairing that DIES to five open mana of creature interaction, when the deck contains a pairing that doesn't, is a sequencing error, not boldness.

The pivot-window rule is written for exactly this moment: 'assume every held answer appears the moment you present lethal, and count the table's open mana before committing.' Five open mana from a proven interaction deck fails that count. This branch becomes correct when the count changes — Player 2 tapped out, or Deflecting Swat free because Edgar is on the battlefield. Naming those preconditions is the point of rejecting it today.

Tutor now, hold the piece hidden; complete it in a dead window

The line
Resolve Black Market Connections. Demonic Tutor for Exquisite Blood and put it in your hand — hidden information. Spend the remaining six mana on unremarkable development: a Vampire or two, each banking an eminence token, while the board's silhouette stays boring. Cast Exquisite Blood on the first turn Player 2 is tapped to two or fewer or has just spent their wrath, then seed the loop with any lifelink connect.
Signals sent
The tutor itself telegraphs — everyone assumes you went for a combo piece — but an assumption is not a target. The tutored card cannot be answered in your hand, and the table's fear now works FOR you: Player 2 must respect the threat every turn, which taxes their mana on defense against a card you choose when to reveal. Expect Vito to eat a removal spell soon; that is a fine trade.
Coalition risk
Moderate and manageable. You are scarier in the abstract but present nothing to unite against. If Vito dies to the panic you induced, they spent a card killing the {2}{B} half — the recastable half — while the seven-mana half stays hidden.
Read

This is threat-deflection played with real cards: 'sequence scary pieces after the table's removal is spent.' And the fetch itself is a real decision, because the deck holds a 2×2 matrix — lose-halves (Vito, Sanguine Bond) × gain-halves (Exquisite Blood, Bloodthirsty Conqueror), all four pairings verified (stacks 001, 004, 005, 006).

Weigh the two serious fetches. Sanguine Bond is the resilient lose-half — stack 005's all-enchantment pairing with Exquisite Blood offers no per-iteration creature-removal window, which is exactly what this table's held interaction profile punishes. But Bond is a second lose-half: with only Vito on board it completes nothing, and you would still need a gain-half later.

Exquisite Blood is a gain-half, and that makes it the pivot card of the whole matrix ON THIS BOARD. It completes with the Vito you already have the moment Player 2 taps low, AND it upgrades into the stack-005 resilient pairing if Vito dies and your second tutor fetches Bond instead of recasting into removal. One fetch, two living partners — and the fragile half of your current pair is the {2}{B} recastable one.

So fetch Exquisite, hold it hidden, and let the panic you induce spend Player 2's removal on Vito at a one-mana-for-one-card exchange rate you happily accept. The enchantment in your hand pairs with the NEXT lose-half either way.

The discipline this line demands is patience under a clock. Natural assembly was never coming (5.4% by turn 6, 9.4% by turn 10 — the tutor IS the assembly), and Player 4's enchantress engine is compounding, so 'a dead window' means the next two or three turns. Name your trigger now: Player 2 taps for their draw engine or wraths, and you combo through the gap that same turn.

Here is how to read the ten below: they are one purchase, made ten ways — redundancy. A singleton format prices consistency two ways, more copies of the job or tutors that fetch it, and this deck already runs the tutors. So the list extends the verified kill-web, adds outlets behind the maindeck's one, and fills the draw and ramp holes the role budget names. Eight of the ten sit in the sideboard, ready to sleeve; two were scouted from the wider pool because they beat everything in the box at their job.

The two scouts are the headline numbers.
